How Many Squares of Shingles for a 1500 Sq Ft Roof

Published August 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

If your home has a 1,500 sq ft footprint, the short answer is that you will order somewhere between 15 and 21 squares of shingles — and the spread is almost entirely down to roof pitch and how much waste the roof shape generates. This guide walks through the square-to-bundle conversion, the pitch multiplier table, and realistic 2026 installed pricing for a roof this size.

Quick answer: A 1,500 sq ft footprint at a 6/12 pitch is about 16.8 squares of roof surface. Add a 10% waste factor and you order roughly 19 squares, which is about 57 bundles of standard shingles. Run your exact numbers through our Shingle Calculator before you buy.

What a roofing "square" actually measures

A square is 100 square feet of finished roof surface — not 100 sq ft of attic floor, and not 100 sq ft of house footprint. That single distinction is where most takeoff errors start. Contractors bid per square because it normalizes material and labor across roofs of different shapes, and shingle manufacturers package to match: standard three-tab and most architectural shingles ship three bundles to the square, so one bundle covers roughly 33.3 sq ft. Heavier laminated or designer profiles are sometimes packaged four bundles per square, so always read the wrapper rather than assuming three.

Weight matters for disposal planning, too. A square of three-tab shingles typically runs 200 to 250 lb, while architectural shingles land closer to 350 to 450 lb per square. On a 19-square job you are ordering somewhere near 4 tons of material, which is why dumpster sizing is a real line item rather than an afterthought.

Why 1,500 sq ft is a footprint, not a roof area

When a homeowner says "1,500 sq ft roof," they almost always mean the footprint — the slab, the attic floor, or the figure on the county assessor record. The actual roof surface is larger, because it slopes. You convert between the two with the pitch multiplier, which comes straight from the Pythagorean theorem.

Pitch multiplier = sqrt(rise^2 + 12^2) / 12 Roof area (sq ft) = footprint area x pitch multiplier Squares = roof area / 100 Bundles = squares x 3 (typical; check the wrapper) Squares to order = squares x (1 + waste factor)

If you do not yet know your pitch, measure it at the rake or gable end with a level and tape, or work it out with the Roof Pitch Calculator, which returns the multiplier and the angle in degrees.

Where the waste factor goes

Nobody orders exactly the geometric area. A simple gable roof with two planes typically needs 10% extra. A hip roof, with four sloping planes and four hip seams, usually runs 15%. Anything with dormers, skylights, or multiple valleys can push past 20%. The extra material covers four things: the starter course along the eaves and rakes, cut shingles at hips and valleys, partial courses where the run does not divide evenly, and the handful of damaged pieces every job produces.

Worked end to end: a 1,500 sq ft footprint at 6/12 gives 16.8 squares. At 10% waste that is 18.5 squares, so you order 19. At three bundles per square, that is 57 bundles. Hip-and-ridge cap is purchased separately — profiles vary, but one bundle of ridge cap typically covers 20 to 35 linear feet, and a 1,500 sq ft hip roof can easily carry 150 linear feet of hip and ridge.

What 19 squares costs in 2026

Installed pricing includes tear-off of one existing layer, synthetic underlayment, ice-and-water shield at eaves and valleys, flashing, labor, and disposal. In 2026, three-tab shingles typically run $350 to $500 per square installed, architectural shingles $450 to $750, and premium designer shingles $700 to $1,200. Coastal metros and the Northeast sit at the top of those ranges; parts of the South and Midwest sit at the bottom.

For a 19-square architectural job, that is roughly $8,550 to $14,250, with a mid-range estimate near $11,400. If you are weighing asphalt against metal, the Metal Roof Calculator will price the standing-seam alternative on the same roof area so the comparison is apples to apples.

Measuring your own roof before you order

Confirm the footprint from the ground or from the assessor record, verify the pitch at the rake, then apply the multiplier. Add overhangs — a 2-ft overhang around a 1,500 sq ft footprint adds meaningful area and is often forgotten. Then round up to the next whole square, because suppliers sell bundles, not fractions.
