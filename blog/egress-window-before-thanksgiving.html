Egress Window Before Thanksgiving? 5.0 Sq Ft, $3,360, 12 Days

Published November 2026 · 9 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A basement sleeping room needs 5.0 square feet of clear opening, and the 32 by 16 slider in a 1968 ranch passes 1.78 with the sash wide open. That is 36 percent of the code minimum, with a sill 10 inches too high. Twelve days is enough for exactly one of the paths below: enlarging the opening you already have, at $1,600 to $3,200. A new cut through the foundation runs $4,200 and takes three to five weeks.

Quick answer: 5.0 square feet clear, 20 inches of clear width, sill no higher than 44 inches. If the room already has a 32 by 16 opening and a well, cut the opening to 36 by 30, drop the sill to 42, and hang a casement. That is $1,600 to $3,200 with the permit, and it can be inspected before the 26th if you file by November 18. A brand new opening in a block wall is $3,800 to $6,200 and will not finish in time. Check the opening size in the Egress Window Calculator before you call a cutter.

Five square feet, measured with the window open

Section R310.2 of the International Residential Code sets the escape opening. A basement sleeping room needs 5.0 square feet of clear opening, at least 20 inches of clear width, at least 24 inches of clear height, and a sill no more than 44 inches above the finished floor. An above-grade bedroom gets 5.7 square feet. The word that carries the argument is clear: it means the hole a person fits through, not the frame the lumberyard sells you.

That distinction kills most basement windows before anyone picks up a saw. A 32 by 16 slider is common in houses built between 1955 and 1980, and only one sash moves. Wide open, the escape hole is 16 by 16 inches, or 256 square inches against the 720 the code asks for.

Sill height is where older basements get caught, and the reason is structural. Frost depth pushes the top of a basement wall up, so the first course that can hold a window sits higher than on a first floor. A 54-inch sill is normal up north. Getting to 42 means cutting block, which is the work nobody budgets for.

What actually passes in a basement wall

Two numbers drive the shopping list: clear opening area and usable wall length. A casement gets the most escape area per inch of wall because the entire sash swings clear of the opening. A slider forfeits half its width every time, and a double hung forfeits half its height.

The 72-inch version clears the bar but needs six uninterrupted feet of foundation wall, and in a 12 by 14 room with a stair landing on one end, that wall often does not exist.

Three paths, priced for a block foundation

Most basements from this era are block, not poured, and that changes the price and the schedule. Block cuts faster, patches with standard units, and rarely needs an engineer. Poured walls need a lintel over the new opening, which is where a $4,200 job turns into an $8,000 one.

The line items behind those totals are predictable. A wet saw cut runs $35 to $60 per linear foot, and a 36 by 30 opening is about 11 feet of cut on both faces, producing roughly 700 pounds of masonry that leaves by bucket. A precast 36 by 36 well runs $700 to $1,400 delivered; a custom one with a drain to the footing tile runs double.

Permits are the part homeowners underestimate. A residential alteration permit for one opening runs $150 to $450 and issues over the counter in three to ten business days, stretching to two weeks in a small city with short inspection staff. Call before you assume.

Weather decides the well, not the wall. A wet saw throws water, and at 28 degrees that water freezes on the basement floor and the apron under the door your mother-in-law will use. Digging in ground that froze on November 12 costs an extra day of breaker work. A well also has to drain before the first hard freeze, or it fills with ice.

Sequence matters if the window shares the list. Cutting dumps block dust into the room, so paint after the cut, never before. The paint timing guide has the cure clock, and the insulation sequence matters if the room is cold as well as non-compliant.

What the inspector measures, in order

Six things get checked, and any one of them can fail the inspection on a job that looks finished.

Clear opening area with the sash fully open, measured at the narrowest point. Sill height to the finished floor, which means the carpet counts, so 44 inches becomes 43 and a half once pad and carpet go down. The well at 9 square feet with 36 inches of projection and width. A permanently attached ladder once the well passes 44 inches deep. Hardware that opens from the inside without a key or a tool. Working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms on the basement level, interconnected with the rest of the house.

Two failures come up most often. A window the previous owner screwed shut to stop a draft, which reads as a blocked escape. And a well cover that needs a tool to lift, common on the polycarbonate covers sold for child safety. Covers are allowed. Covers that cannot be pushed open from underneath are not.

When the honest answer is the upstairs bedroom

A rec room does not need an egress window. A bedroom does. The gap between those two words is where most of the money and most of the argument lives, because a room labeled storage that holds a bed is a bedroom to an appraiser, to a buyer's inspector, and to a tenant's lawyer.

If the room is used twelve nights a year, the arithmetic is hard to defend. $3,360 buys one compliant opening and a room that shows up as a bedroom on the listing when you sell. If it holds a sectional the other 353 days, put the guests upstairs, spend $180 on interconnected alarms, and revisit this in April. The guest bedroom conversion numbers cover the rest of that project, and the floor load check matters before a bed and dresser land down there.

Thanksgiving egress questions

Does a basement room need an egress window if guests only sleep there twice a year?

The code follows the room's use, not the calendar. A space built and labeled as a sleeping room needs the opening whether it is occupied nightly or twice a year, and the requirement shows up at permit, at inspection, and at resale. A rec room with a futon sits in a gray area that stops being gray once the room is listed as a bedroom or rented out.

Can I widen the existing opening instead of cutting a new one?

Usually, if the wall is block and the opening has room to grow in both directions. Most inspectors want a full course of block between the new opening and the corner, with the sill course above the footing left intact. Going from 32 by 16 to 36 by 30 stays inside one bay in most 8-inch block walls. A saw cutter confirms it in ten minutes, and that visit costs less than the permit.

How deep can a window well be before it needs a ladder?

Forty-four inches. Below that the well is shallow enough to climb out of; above it the code wants a permanently attached ladder or steps that do not obstruct the window. Most precast wells land between 48 and 60 inches deep, so most of them need the ladder, and the $60 to $150 ladder is the cheapest item on the job. Skip it and the inspection fails in the last five minutes of a three-week project.

The call

Enlarge the opening you have. Filing the permit today puts a cutter in the basement Monday, an inspection on the 23rd, and a compliant room with a week to spare. Budget $3,360 and treat anything under $2,800 as a quote worth reading closely, since a cutter who skips the permit leaves you with an opening nobody signed off on.

The trade-off is real. Cutting block in a finished basement makes dust that finds the ceiling joists and stays there, and the patch outside will never match 1970s mortar. You are spending $3,360 on a window that gets opened approximately never. What you get is a room that can legally be called a bedroom, a guest who leaves through a 36-inch hole instead of a 16-inch one, and one less line on the buyer's inspection report. If none of that is worth $3,360 this month, the upstairs bedroom is a legitimate answer and the cut waits for April.
