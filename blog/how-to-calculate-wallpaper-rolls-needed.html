How to Calculate Wallpaper Rolls Needed

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Wallpaper is sold by the roll and hung by the strip, and those two units do not line up neatly. That mismatch is why so many projects end with a frantic hunt for one more roll of a discontinued pattern. The fix is a short calculation that counts strips rather than guessing at square footage.

Quick answer: Total the wall area, divide by the usable coverage of one roll — typically about 45 to 50 square feet for a standard double roll once trimming and pattern matching are accounted for — and round up. A 10 by 12 foot room with 8 foot ceilings usually needs 7 to 9 double rolls. Put your own numbers through the Wallpaper Calculator.

Step 1: measure wall area, not floor area

Measure the perimeter of the room and multiply by the ceiling height. A 10 by 12 foot room has a 44 foot perimeter, so 44 feet times 8 feet gives 352 square feet of wall. Subtract large openings only: a standard door is roughly 20 square feet and a window 15 to 20. Outlets, switches and small vents remove so little that they are not worth adjusting for, and the leftover few square feet are useful slack when a corner is out of square.

Step 2: read the real coverage off the roll label

Rolls are labeled with a width, a length and a total square footage, and those numbers vary more than most people expect. Use the label rather than a rule of thumb, because roll sizes are not standardized worldwide.

The gap between total and usable coverage is where most estimates go wrong. A roll that claims 56 square feet rarely papers 56 square feet of wall unless the pattern is a random match and the ceiling height divides evenly into the roll length.

Step 3: allow for the pattern repeat

The pattern repeat is the distance before the design lines up again, printed on the label together with the match type. A straight match lines up horizontally and a drop match is offset by half a repeat, which wastes noticeably more paper.

Repeat size and matchTypical wasteExtra rolls, 10x12 room
Random / free match0-5%None
Up to 6 in, straightabout 8%0-1
7 to 12 in, straightabout 12%1
13 to 24 inabout 18%1
25 in and up, drop match25-30%1-2

Multiply the wall area by one plus the waste percentage before dividing by coverage. On a large-format mural with a 30 percent allowance, a 352 square foot room becomes roughly 458 square feet of paper to buy, which is the difference between eight rolls and ten.

Step 4: convert area into rolls with the strip method

A strip calculation is more accurate than pure square footage because it rounds down how many drops you can actually cut from each roll. It takes about two extra minutes and it is the method professionals use.

Wall area = Perimeter x Ceiling height - Openings Strips needed = Wall width / Roll width (round up) Drop length = Ceiling height + trim allowance (2-4 in) + Pattern repeat Strips per roll = Roll length / Drop length (round down) Rolls = Strips needed / Strips per roll (round up)

Work the 10 by 12 example: 44 feet of wall, a 20.5 inch roll, and a 12 inch repeat with a straight match. Each drop needs 8 feet of height, plus 3 inches of trim allowance, plus 12 inches of repeat, or about 9.25 feet. A 33 foot roll yields three drops. Dividing 44 feet by 1.71 feet of roll width gives 26 strips, and 26 divided by three drops per roll is 8.67, so buy 9 double rolls. The square-foot method would have said 8 — exactly the roll you would run out on.

Doors, windows and awkward walls

Openings are worth subtracting, but not always. On a wall that is almost entirely glass, removing the window drops the strip count by one or two and can save a roll. On a wall with a centered door you still need full-height drops on both sides, so the nominal 20 square feet disappears into the waste. Subtract for real only on wide pass-throughs and oversized picture windows where a whole drop is genuinely not needed.

Ordering rules that save a second trip

  • Buy every roll at once. Dye lots differ between production batches, and a mismatch is visible on a seam in daylight.
  • Add one spare roll. Ten percent extra covers a future patch, and discontinued patterns cannot be matched later.
  • Check the return policy. Most US retailers accept unopened rolls within 30 days; opened or cut rolls are almost never returnable.
  • Prep the wall first. A sizing coat keeps paste from soaking in and makes future removal easier.
  • Peel-and-stick follows the same math but cannot be repositioned after the first press, so buy the spare roll before you start the first wall.

Turning rolls into a budget

Mid-range wallpaper in 2026 runs roughly $30 to $80 per double roll, with designer and grasscloth patterns well above that, and professional installation adds about $1.50 to $4.00 per square foot of wall, or $400 to $900 for a standard bedroom.
