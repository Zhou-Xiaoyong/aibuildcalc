How Many Lumens for a 12-Person Thanksgiving Table? 3,000, at 32 Inches

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Twelve people at a table stretched to 144 inches need about 3,000 lumens of fixture output, hung 32 inches above the cloth. That is not one chandelier. A 30-inch six-light fixture sized for the original 8-foot table lights the middle eight seats and leaves the four end chairs at 8 to 12 footcandles, which is enough to see a plate and not enough to see what is on it.

Quick answer: 42 sq ft of table at 30 footcandles is 1,260 lumens landing on the cloth, which takes roughly 3,000 lumens out of the fixture once shade loss and spill to the ceiling are counted. A six-light chandelier with 470-lumen candelabra LEDs runs $250 to $550, an LED-compatible dimmer adds $150 to $250 installed, and a new ceiling box in a dining room that never had one costs $850 to $1,800 when there is no attic above it. Run your own room through the Lighting Calculator before you order a fixture.

Twelve chairs turn an 8-foot table into 12 feet

An 8-foot table seats eight, four to a side at 24 inches per person. A 48-inch folding table pushed onto one end makes it 144 inches and twelve chairs, six to a side, and every host in the country does it the same way: the seam lands just off center and the centerpiece gets pushed over it.

The chandelier does not move. It stays on the box it was hung from, centered over the original 96 inches, so the geometry of the room quietly changes under a light that was sized for a smaller meal.

November makes that matter more than it would in June. Sunset on the 26th runs from 4:25 in Chicago to 5:30 in Atlanta, which means a two o'clock dinner finishes under fixture light alone. There is no window assist at the end of the meal and no daylight in the room when the pie comes out.

Thirty footcandles over the cloth, twenty for the room

Our Lighting Calculator carries four room tiers: kitchen 50 footcandles, office 40, garage 30, living 20. A dining room falls in the living tier for general light at 20 footcandles, and that is where people stop and get the answer wrong. The table itself is a work surface for one hour a day, and carving a bird at the 20-footcandle room level is how someone ends up cutting toward a finger.

Plan 30 footcandles on the tabletop, 20 for the walls. The fixture over the table is what delivers the difference, and the conversion from footcandles to lumens is the only arithmetic in this article.

Two assumptions drive that 0.41. A chandelier is not a downlight: roughly half of what it makes goes up to the ceiling and out to the walls, and glass shades and fabric diffusers eat another quarter. Change the fixture type and the factor changes with it, which is why a recessed can rated 800 lumens delivers closer to 600 on the table while a bare candelabra bulb in open air gets most of its 470 there.

A 144-inch table is the one exception most houses hit once a year, and it is the length where a single round fixture stops working as a lighting plan and starts working as decoration.

One chandelier covers the middle eight feet

At 32 inches above the cloth, a 30-inch chandelier puts 15 footcandles or better on a pool roughly 8 feet across. Illuminance falls off with the square of the distance to the tabletop, so the edges of the pool drop fast: 40 footcandles under the fixture and 15 at four feet out is normal for 2,800 lumens.

On a 96-inch table that pool covers almost the whole surface and nobody notices. On a 144-inch table it covers the middle 96 inches, four of the six chairs on each side, and the two chairs at each end sit outside it.

The two end chairs on each side sit at 8 to 12 footcandles. That is dim enough that the cranberry sauce reads as dark red paste and bright enough that nobody complains out loud.

Two chandeliers is the right answer for the room and the wrong answer for November, because the second box is a cutting job on a ceiling that then needs patching and paint that will not be fully cured by the 26th. Read the cure clock in the guest room paint breakdown before you decide otherwise; latex needs days, not hours, before you hang something against it and call it done.

Hang height comes off the cloth, not the floor

Thirty to thirty-six inches above the tabletop is the range that keeps a seated person's sightline across the table open. Measured from the floor it is meaningless, because table heights differ: a dining table is 30 inches, most folding tables are 29, and a counter-height setup is 36. Measure off the taller of the two tops when the table has been extended, or the last two feet of the meal sit somewhere between the range and the floor.

On an 8-foot ceiling the arithmetic is unforgiving. Ninety-six inches minus a 30-inch tabletop leaves 66 inches between the cloth and the ceiling, so the fixture body, canopy and chain together have to add up to 30 to 36 inches and no more. A 24-inch body with a 2-inch canopy drops 26 inches and parks the bottom at 40 inches above the cloth, which is why so many dining fixtures look perched: the chain was left in the box.

Eight inches of chain puts the same fixture at 32 inches and back inside the range. Chain costs about $2 a foot, and a longer chain is the only thing standing between a $300 fixture and a room that photographs like a showroom.

With a 9-foot ceiling, the gap becomes 78 inches and the same fixture needs 16 to 22 inches of chain. If the ceiling is vaulted over the table, measure the height at the low side, because that is the point closest to the diners no matter what the peak says.

The box is the expensive part, and the circuit is not

A 3,000-lumen fixture is about 30 watts of LED. On a 15-amp, 120-volt circuit that is 0.25 amps, which is nothing, and the Thanksgiving kitchen load is a far bigger number than anything a chandelier adds. The Circuit Load Calculator will confirm you have room, and it will not be the reason this job costs money.

The reason is the box. NEC 210.70 requires a wall-switch-controlled lighting outlet in kitchens, bedrooms, bathrooms, hallways, stairways and attached garages, and dining rooms are not on that list. Plenty of houses built between 1950 and 1980 have a switched receptacle in the dining room and no ceiling outlet at all, because that is what the code allowed and what builders sold.

  • Box present, attic above: $350 to $850 for a new box and switch leg, half a day, no patch.
  • Box present, no attic above it: $850 to $1,800, because the cut, the fish, the patch and the paint are the job.
  • No box and no attic: the same $850 to $1,800 plus a receptacle-fed swag as the fallback, or a surface raceway that nobody wants over a Thanksgiving table.
  • Fixture weight: most ceiling boxes are listed for luminaires up to 50 lb, and anything heavier or ceiling-suspended has to go on a box marked for the load. A 12 lb chandelier is fine on what is there. A 40 lb wrought iron piece on a plastic nail-on box is not, and the fix is a fan-rated brace, not a longer screw.

Adding a dedicated 15-amp circuit for a 30-watt fixture is money spent on nothing. If you are already opening the ceiling, the Wire Gauge Calculator is the tool for the switch leg you are actually pulling, not for capacity you do not need.

2,700 K, CRI 90, and the phone camera

Two numbers decide whether the table looks like a magazine or a cafeteria. Color temperature should be 2,700 K to 3,000 K. A 4,000 K lamp over a dining table turns turkey skin gray and makes the room read as a break room, and it is the single most common mistake in a fixture bought online without reading the bulb spec.

Color rendering should be 90 or better, and the R9 value matters more than the headline CRI: R9 is the red channel, and cranberry sauce, rare meat and red wine all live there. A CRI 80 lamp has R9 near zero, which is why the food photographs flat no matter what phone you use.

Dimming is the last piece. Six dimmable candelabra LEDs on a dimmer rated for LED loads will dim cleanly to about 10 percent. Six on an old incandescent dimmer will buzz, flicker, or drop out below 30 percent, and the camera sees the flicker as banding even when your eye does not. Buy the dimmer from the fixture maker's compatibility list when it publishes one, and skip the $18 dimmer with no list at all.

What to buy, in the order you will use it

Count the sockets in the fixture you already own before anything else. Six sockets at 470 lumens is 2,800 lumens, which is 94 percent of the 3,000 target for $36 in bulbs and no electrical work. That is the cheapest improvement available this week and most people skip straight to a new fixture.

Second, the dimmer. A $45 LED-compatible dimmer plus $150 to $200 of an electrician's time turns a bright room into a room that can be bright at four and low at seven, which is worth more on the day than another 400 lumens.

Third, the fixture, and only if the existing one is smaller than 26 inches over a 96-inch table. Everything else waits for January, when the ceiling can be opened, patched and painted without a deadline attached to it.

Thanksgiving table lighting questions

How many lumens for a dining table that seats eight?

About 2,000 lumens of fixture output for a 96-inch table at 30 footcandles on the surface. A six-light chandelier with 470-lumen candelabra LEDs gets there with a little margin, which is why the 26-to-30-inch six-light is the most common dining fixture in the country.

Can I hang a chandelier where there is no ceiling box?

Not safely. A canopy has to mount to a box listed for the fixture's weight, and drywall anchors in half-inch board will not hold a 12 lb fixture over a table where people are sitting. The honest short-term options are a plug-in swag on a hook rated for the load, or table lamps on the sideboard, with the box scheduled for after the holiday.

Will a bigger chandelier trip the circuit?

No. A 3,000-lumen LED fixture draws about 0.25 amps on a 15-amp circuit, and even a 12-lamp incandescent chandelier at 60 watts each draws 6 amps, well under the 12-amp continuous limit. The constraint on a bigger fixture is the box and the ceiling, never the breaker.

The call

Keep the fixture you have, put six 470-lumen 2,700 K candelabra LEDs in it and add an LED-compatible dimmer. That is $80 in parts and an evening, it lands you at 2,800 lumens on a 42 sq ft table, and it leaves the ceiling closed with seven days to spare.

The trade-off is the four end chairs, which sit at 8 to 12 footcandles and will stay there. Carve at the middle of the table and put the person who wants to read the fine print on the bird next to the centerpiece. If the room is going to host twelve every year, the 60-inch linear pendant at $320 to $1,100 plus a second box is the real fix, and it is a January job with a paint can and a Saturday in it. The electrical calculators cover the circuit side; the light itself is a fixture, a bulb and a dimmer, in that order.
