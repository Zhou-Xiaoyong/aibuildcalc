How Many Amps Does Thanksgiving Dinner Pull? 26 on a 20-Amp Circuit

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 20-pound turkey in a 1,650-watt roaster pulls 13.75 amps. Put the coffee maker beside it and the pair draws 26.25 amps through a 20-amp circuit that is only rated to carry 16 of them continuously. That is the four minutes before dinner when half the kitchen goes dark and somebody starts opening cabinet doors looking for the panel.

Quick answer: a normal Thanksgiving peak is 39.6 amps across the kitchen counter, and the two 20-amp small-appliance circuits required by the code carry 32 amps continuously. One dedicated 20-amp countertop circuit costs $280 to $650 installed where a basement or crawl space is open, and it is the one electrical upgrade that still fits in the 18 days before November 26. Thanksgiving is the only day the kitchen load matters

Most kitchens run two heating appliances at once on a bad night. Thursday runs five: a roaster holding a bird for four hours, a wall oven cycling, a microwave reheating something, a coffee maker on its second pot, and a stand mixer whipping cream because someone forgot the pie. All of them are 120-volt plug loads sharing two circuits.

The rest of the year nothing overlaps long enough to heat a breaker. Thanksgiving is a six-hour event with a deadline. The house was wired for a Tuesday.

None of this is a service problem. A 200-amp panel has 48,000 watts behind it. The bottleneck is the last 25 feet of 12-gauge wire between the panel and the counter.

The load, appliance by appliance

Nameplate wattage divided by 120 volts gives amps. Resistive heating elements run at a power factor near 1, so the arithmetic is honest for a roaster, a griddle or a coffee maker. Motors and electronics draw slightly more than the label suggests, which is why the mixer number below is conservative.

Everything at once totals 8,700 watts, or 72.5 amps. Nobody runs all eight. The realistic 1:40 p.m. overlap is roaster, coffee maker, microwave and mixer: 4,750 watts, 39.6 amps, spread across two circuits that together carry 32.

The 80 percent rule is what actually trips it

A 20-amp breaker does not carry 20 amps forever. The code sizes branch circuits at 125 percent of the continuous load, which leaves 80 percent usable. Twenty amps becomes 16. Fifteen becomes 12. That is the number on the table, and it is why a 1,650-watt roaster on a 15-amp general-purpose circuit is already over the line before anything else is plugged in.

A breaker does not trip the instant it sees 26 amps. Thermal-magnetic breakers tolerate roughly twice their rating for a minute or more, which is exactly why the power stays on through the carving and drops during the dishwashing. Four fixes, priced for 2026

An electrician runs $95 to $150 an hour with a two-hour minimum in most markets, and a permit for one circuit is $60 to $150 depending on the jurisdiction. Those two numbers explain the spread below.

The cheap fix is genuinely cheap and often enough. Find the dining room receptacle, trip breakers one at a time until it goes dead, and see whether it shares a circuit with the counter. In a lot of houses built before 1990 it does, which means moving the roaster there changes nothing. Test it before you count on it.

Households roasting a bird while a second oven runs usually land at the $420 middle of that first row: a 20-amp circuit, 12-gauge copper, a GFCI receptacle and an AFCI breaker. Both protections are required on kitchen countertops under the 2020 code cycle, and most jurisdictions have adopted it.

Eighteen days is enough for one circuit, not for a panel

Today is November 8. Guests arrive the 26th, which leaves twelve working days and three weekends. One circuit fits. A service upgrade does not.

  • This week: call two electricians and ask for a dedicated 20-amp countertop circuit with permit. November is not their busy season, but the pre-holiday window is.
  • Days 1 to 4: permit over the counter, then four hours of work. The panel cover comes off, a knockout gets a new breaker, and the cable runs to a new box above the counter.
  • Days 5 to 8: inspection. Some jurisdictions combine rough and final for one circuit.
  • By November 20: done, with six days of slack for a failed inspection or a backordered breaker.

A panel upgrade runs into a utility disconnect, a new meter base and a scheduled outage. Four to six weeks on a good month, so book it for February if the panel is genuinely full.

Map your circuits this weekend for $45

You cannot fix an overload you cannot see. A plug-in circuit tracer costs $40 to $60 and sends a tone through the wiring that the receiver picks up at the panel. Fifteen minutes with one produces a labeled panel and a list of which outlet belongs to which breaker.

Do that before buying anything else. A $30 plug-in watt meter reads actual draw and settles the arguments a nameplate starts. Whatever else you buy, buy a 12-gauge cord and skip the 16-gauge one in the garage: the Wire Gauge Calculator shows how fast a thin cord at 25 feet turns 13.75 amps into a warm jacket and a 4 percent drop.

What not to do on Thursday

Do not replace the 20-amp breaker with a 30. The breaker protects the 12-gauge wire in the wall, not the roaster, and that wire is rated for 20 amps. A 30-amp breaker on 12-gauge cable turns a nuisance trip into a fire inside a wall cavity, and an inspector will find it the moment you sell.

Do not put two heating appliances on one power strip. A strip rated 15 amps with a roaster and a coffee maker on it is carrying 26, and the plastic fails first. Plug heat directly into the wall.

Do not run the roaster to the garage on a long cord in 35-degree weather. Cold makes the jacket brittle, the run is usually 14-gauge at best, and a garage receptacle often shares the opener and the freezer.

If the wall oven is electric, check the panel before assuming you need capacity. Two breakers ganged together with a 40 or 50 on the handle means the oven already has its own 240-volt circuit.

Thanksgiving kitchen electrical questions

Why does the breaker trip after dinner instead of during?

Because that is when the load stacks up. The roaster finishes and the dishwasher starts, the coffee maker goes on for a second pot, someone puts the slow cooker back on warm. A thermal breaker takes minutes to react to a moderate overload, so the trip lands in the last hour of the meal rather than at the start.

Can I just run an extension cord from the dining room?

Sometimes. Use a 12-gauge cord no longer than 25 feet and confirm first that the dining receptacle is not already on a kitchen small-appliance circuit, because the code restricts those circuits to kitchen, dining and pantry receptacles and builders often share them. A lamp cord is not an option at 13.75 amps.

Does a 1,650-watt roaster need its own circuit?

On a 15-amp circuit it should not be used at all. It draws 13.75 amps against a 12-amp continuous rating, so the breaker is over its limit with nothing else running. On a 20-amp circuit it fits with 2.25 amps to spare, which is roughly one slow cooker and no coffee maker.

The call

Book one electrician this week for a dedicated 20-amp countertop circuit. At $420 in a house with an open basement it is the cheapest capacity you will ever buy, it clears inspection before the 20th, and it fixes the problem permanently instead of for one Thursday.

The trade-off is where the cable lands. If the panel sits across a finished kitchen, you pay for a patch and a paint touch-up on top of the circuit, and the two-coat patch routine is a Saturday you did not plan to spend. Skip the panel upgrade until February and skip the 30-amp breaker entirely. If the budget is zero this month, buy the $45 tracer, map the panel, and keep the coffee maker off the circuit the microwave is on.
