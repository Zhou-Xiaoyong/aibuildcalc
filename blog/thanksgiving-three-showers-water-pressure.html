Three Showers at Once on Thanksgiving: How Much Water Pressure Is Left? 32 PSI

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 2,400 sq ft colonial with a 3/4-inch copper main reads 44 psi at the second-floor shower head with every tap closed. Three showers running together leave 32 psi at that head, down 12 psi from where the needle sits when the house is quiet. Add a washing machine fill and the third shower falls to 12.

Quick answer: flowing pressure is static pressure minus 4.33 psi for every 10 feet of rise, minus meter loss, minus friction, minus the valve and head. On this house that is 68 psi at the street, 52 after the regulator, 44 upstairs with nothing running, and 32 with three showers going. Three 1.75 gpm heads cut the peak draw from 7.5 gpm to 5.25 and put 37 psi back at the head for about $90. Thanksgiving morning is the worst 40 minutes of the year for water pressure

Most houses run one shower on a weekday. Thursday puts three adults in three bathrooms inside the same half hour, with the water heater still recovering and the dishwasher on its final rinse. Whatever margin the system had in October gets spent between 7:00 and 7:40.

Demand is set at the head, not the pipe. Federal law caps a showerhead at 2.5 gpm at 80 psi, unchanged since the 1992 Energy Policy Act took effect in 1994. Three of them ask for 7.5 gallons a minute, and the 3/4-inch main has to deliver it. The house here is a 1996 colonial: 3.5 baths, a 5/8-inch meter, 62 feet of developed copper to the upstairs riser, and a regulator at the mechanical tee.

The regulator is the line most people miss. It protects fixtures from street pressure above 80 psi, and it does that by throwing away 16 psi of margin in a house that never needed to lose it. A regulator that has sagged to 50 moves every number in that table down by two.

Elevation is the other fixed cost. About 4.33 psi disappears per 10 feet of rise regardless of pipe size, so a basement shower and a second-floor shower in the same house sit 8 psi apart. If the complaint is only about the upstairs bath, that is usually the whole explanation.

Loss through a meter and a pipe run tracks the square of flow, so the second shower costs more than the first.

That last row ruins a morning. Twelve psi is above the 8 psi floor the code sets at a flush-tank fixture, so nothing is technically broken. It is well under the 20 psi most pressure-balancing valves want at both inlets, which is why the third shower swings hot and cold instead of just running slow. The $90 fix that buys back 5 psi

You cannot add pressure on a Thursday. You can lower demand, the one variable in that budget a homeowner controls without a permit. Three heads at 1.75 gpm drop peak draw from 7.5 gallons a minute to 5.25, and because loss follows the square of flow the penalty shrinks from 10.2 psi to 5.0. The third shower goes from 32 to 37.

Heads run $25 to $60 and most swap with bare hands. Buy the ones with a fixed restrictor rather than the removable plug, because the plug is the first thing a guest pulls when the spray feels thin.

Two free moves do nearly as much. Keep the washing machine out of the 7:00 to 8:00 window, which protects the worst case from 12 psi, and stagger showers twenty minutes apart so no more than two heads run together. At two, the head reads 38 psi and nobody notices.

The trade-off is real: 1.75 gpm feels thinner than 2.5 on the first shower. It is also the only honest choice, because a 2.5 gpm head only passes 2.5 gpm at 80 psi. At 32 psi it passes about 1.6, so the three-shower draw settles nearer 5 gpm than 7.5. You are not losing water you had.

The 20-minute checks before you blame the pipes

  • Put a gauge on a hose bib. Twenty dollars buys one with a hose thread. Sixty psi or better at rest, and a drop under 20 percent with a tap wide open, means the system is fine and the complaint is about simultaneous use.
  • Look at the main shutoff. A gate valve closed for a repair last spring and opened three-quarters of the way is the most common restriction in an older house, and finishing the turn costs nothing.
  • Check the regulator setting. Most adjust from 25 to 75 psi under a lock nut on top. Moving 45 to 55 buys 10 psi everywhere, and the ceiling is 80, which is what fixtures are rated to.
  • Clean the aerators. Grit from a main break packs the screen, and the symptom is one weak faucet rather than a weak house.
  • Test hot and cold separately. Weak on the hot side points at the dip tube or a clogged nipple. The 50-gallon recovery math covers the volume side.

What does not save pressure

Removing aerators raises flow at one fixture and lowers pressure at every other one, since the gallons come out of the same main. Turning up the water heater changes temperature, not pressure. A tankless swap does nothing for pressure and can make it worse when a 1.75 gpm head falls below the unit's activation flow.

Showerheads sold as high pressure are a restrictor with a marketing sticker. They add demand to the same pipe, and the second shower pays.

The 1-inch main question

If the gauge reads 60 at rest and the shower still collapses, the trunk is undersized and no showerhead fixes it. Copper and PEX are not interchangeable at the same nominal size. Three-quarter Type L copper has a 0.785-inch bore; three-quarter PEX has 0.671. At 7.5 gpm that is 5.0 feet per second against 6.8, and above 8 you hear the pipe and start eroding fittings.

A 60-foot 1-inch trunk runs $700 to $1,600 with the basement ceiling open. A whole-house repipe in 2,400 sq ft runs $4,500 to $8,500. That is a spring project.

Thanksgiving water pressure questions

Why does the shower go cold when someone flushes?

The valve is doing its job. A pressure-balancing cartridge senses the cold-side drop and throttles hot to match, so a 30 psi swing pulls the hot side down with it. At 12 psi total the cartridge runs out of travel and the temperature hunts.

Will a bigger water heater fix the pressure?

No. Tank size sets how many gallons you have, not how fast they arrive. It fixes the fourth person's cold shower and leaves the third person's weak spray alone. The bathroom load math covers the fixture-count side.

Is 32 psi enough for a shower?

Legal and thin. Codes put the floor at 8 psi at a flush-tank fixture and 15 at a flushometer valve; most people call 40 to 60 comfortable and fixtures are rated to 80. At 32 a standard head works and a rain head does not.

The call

Buy three 1.75 gpm heads and a $20 gauge tonight, then keep the laundry out of the 7 a.m. hour. That is $110 and twenty minutes, and it moves the third shower from 32 psi to 37 without touching a pipe.

The trade-off: you give up spray volume on every shower for the other 364 days to protect one morning. If the gauge shows 60 at rest and the drop stays past 20 percent after the regulator check, the answer is a 1-inch trunk in the spring, not another showerhead.
