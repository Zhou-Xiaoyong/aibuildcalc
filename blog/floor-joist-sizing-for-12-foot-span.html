Floor Joist Sizing for 12 Foot Span

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 24 by 12 ft bonus room over a garage takes 19 joists. The 2x8 that showed up on the plan will not carry that 12 ft at 16 in on center, and the 2x10 that will costs $61 more for the whole floor.

Quick answer: frame a 12 ft clear span with 2x10 at 16 in on center in No. 2 SPF, or 2x8 at 12 in on center if depth is tight. A 2x8 at 16 in stops near 11 ft 6 in. Buy 14 ft stock: 12 ft of clear span plus 1-1/2 in of bearing at each end is 12 ft 3 in. The Floor Joist Calculator turns a room size into a count and a linear-foot order.

Twelve feet is where 2x8 stops

Span is measured between the faces of the supports, not center to center. A room that measures 12 ft inside gets a 12 ft clear span and a joist longer than 12 ft.

In No. 2 SPF a 2x8 tops out near 11 ft 6 in at 16 in on center, under the 40 psf live and 10 psf dead load the code assumes for a habitable room. At 12 in on center the same joist reaches 13 ft 3 in. Twelve feet falls in the gap between those numbers, which is why the question gets asked.

Clear span = face of support to face of support Joists = (room length in inches / spacing) + 1 Joist length = clear span + 3 in for bearing Stock = next 2 ft length above joist length 24 x 12 ft room, 16 in on center: 288 / 16 = 18 spaces, 19 joists 144 + 3 = 147 in = 12 ft 3 in, buy 14 ft 19 x 14 = 266 linear ft

The 2x10 at 24 in on center is the trap: an inch under the line, passing inspection, still feeling like a trampoline mid-room.

Every value assumes the stamp reads No. 2. Southern Pine runs about 12 percent longer at the same size, most of the way to the next depth up, and Douglas fir-larch buys an inch or two. None of it means anything until you read the stamp.

The 2x10 layout wins on stiffness per dollar and frames faster with six fewer joists and a quarter fewer nails. The 2x8 route is $61 cheaper in lumber and hands most of it back in labor.

Species and grade move the number more than depth

A 2x10 in Southern Pine No. 2 reaches about 16 ft 4 in at 16 in on center, where the same board in SPF stops at 14 ft 8 in. Twenty inches from a species swap, at no cost.

Grade is the other lever: No. 1 buys 6 to 12 in over No. 2 for 15 to 25 percent more a board. Stud grade is not a floor joist grade, whatever the pile at the end of the aisle suggests. Kiln-dried stock stamped KD19 is what the table assumes, and treated lumber over a crawl space falls under the wet-service table, which cuts the span.

Where the table stops and the floor still bounces

L/360 is the floor the code sets, not a comfort target. A 14 ft span is allowed 0.47 in of live-load deflection, and a floor that moves nearly half an inch reads as soft no matter what the inspection card says.

Under tile the bar rises. Ceramic wants L/360, stone wants L/720, and both want a second layer of plywood or an uncoupling membrane. Hardwood is nailed through the subfloor into the joists, so 16 in on center is the practical limit for 3/4 in strip; at 24 in it needs sleepers. The Hardwood Floor Calculator covers coverage once the frame is settled.

Notches and holes ruin sound floors after inspection. IRC R502.8.1 caps a notch at one-sixth of the depth, bans notches in the middle third of the span, and caps a hole at one-third of the depth with 2 in of wood at the top and bottom edge. A 2x10 is 9-1/4 in deep, so the biggest hole through it is 3 in and it has to sit in the middle 5-1/4 in.

What to order

Order 19 pieces of 2x10 by 14 ft, No. 2 or better, glue and screw 3/4 in tongue-and-groove subfloor to it, and the frame passes the table with 2 ft 8 in of margin.

The 2x8 at 12 in on center is a legitimate second choice at $61 less in lumber. It costs six more joists to cut and hang, and a third more midspan deflection.

The trade-off on the deeper joist is headroom. A 2x10 hangs 2 in lower than a 2x8, which matters over a basement stair and not at all in a garage ceiling. When the span has to stay shallow, keep the 2x8 at 12 in. The rest of the flooring calculators cover the finish above the frame.
