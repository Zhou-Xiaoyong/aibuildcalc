Concrete for a Front Walk Before Thanksgiving: 0.74 Cubic Yards and a 15-Day Clock

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 3-foot by 20-foot walk at 4 inches thick holds 20 cubic feet of concrete: 0.74 cubic yards, or 37 eighty-pound bags once waste is counted. Fourteen guests will not crack a slab that is two weeks old. A 28°F night in its first 24 hours will, and that is the deadline nobody prices in.

Quick answer: budget 0.81 cubic yards for a 3 by 20 foot walk with a 10% waste allowance. That is 37 bags of 80-pound mix, about $220 to $300 in material, or one short-load ready-mix delivery. Pour by November 18 and the slab is walkable, broom-swept and out of the way before the 26th. Size your own dimensions in the Concrete Slab Calculator first; the takeoff takes under a minute.

The takeoff, before you call anybody

Measure the finished surface, not the hole you dig for it. A walk running 3 feet wide and 20 feet from the driveway apron to the stoop is 60 square feet. Four inches is the standard residential thickness; go to 5 inches where a lawn mower or a delivery hand truck crosses, and 4 inches is already enough for a person in boots.

The math is length times width times thickness in feet, then divide by 27 to get yards. That is 20 cubic feet, or 0.74 yards at full depth. Add 10% when the forms curve or the grade steps down toward the street, and the order becomes 0.81 yards. The concrete volume walkthrough covers the same formula for larger pours, including why irregular shapes need 15%.

Front walk, 3 ft x 20 ft at 4 in thickness in feet = 4 / 12 = 0.333 cubic feet = 3 x 20 x 0.333 = 20.0 cubic yards = 20.0 / 27 = 0.74 With 10% waste for forms and a curved end 0.74 x 1.10 = 0.81 cu yd = 22.0 cu ft Bag count, wet yield per bag 80 lb bag = 0.60 cu ft -> 22.0 / 0.60 = 37 bags 60 lb bag = 0.45 cu ft -> 22.0 / 0.45 = 49 bags mixed weight = 22.0 x 145 lb/cu ft = 3,190 lb Add a 4 x 4 landing at the door 5.3 cu ft = 0.20 cu yd = 9 more 80 lb bags Strength gain, Type I mix held at 70 F day 1 ~16% day 7 ~70% day 3 ~40% day 14 ~88% day 28 = 100%

Bag counts are where small pours go wrong. An 80-pound bag yields about 0.6 cubic feet of mixed concrete, so 0.81 yards is 37 bags and roughly 3,200 pounds of wet material. That is only four wheelbarrow loads at 6 cubic feet each, which surprises people who assume a truck saves a day of pushing. What the truck actually saves is mixing time: 37 bags by hand is four to six hours for two people, and the first batch starts setting while you are still opening bag twenty.

Why the 26th turns a walkway into a project

Every guest uses that walk, and most of them use it after dark carrying a covered dish. Two defects cause the fall that shows up in family stories: a joint that has heaved a half inch proud of its neighbor, and a slab that broke away where the walk meets the driveway apron and left a 2-inch lip.

Width matters more than people expect at a holiday. Thirty-six inches is the usual minimum for a residential walk, and 48 inches is what you want where two people pass, or where anyone uses a walker or a wheelchair. If your walk is 30 inches and the party is 14 people, the bottleneck on Thanksgiving is not the concrete, it is that narrow strip and the mud beside it.

The deicer question arrives with the first cold forecast. Sodium chloride and calcium chloride both scale concrete in its first winter, and a slab poured in November gets the worst possible version of that test. Sand or grit does the same job on the ice and leaves the surface alone. The salt does not hurt the slab structurally; it takes the top sixteenth of an inch of finished surface with it, which you will notice next spring as a ring of exposed aggregate at the door.

Read the last row before the first three. If the walk is sound except for one lifted panel and a broken apron edge, a patch is the correct November answer, and it is the only one of the four that is finished and cured by the 20th without weather risk. Small flatwork is also priced by the minimum, not by the square foot: a 60 square foot walk costs nearly what a 200 square foot walk costs because the crew, the truck, and the trip are identical. That is why $17 to $30 per square foot shows up on small jobs while a 400 square foot pour lands near $9.

The cure clock and the pour dates that still work

ACI 306 defines cold weather as an average daily temperature below 40°F for three days running, and once you are in that window the rules change: concrete placed at 50°F minimum, held above 50 for the first 48 hours, and protected from freezing until it reaches about 500 psi. Never place on frozen subgrade. A 4-inch slab is thin enough that ground frost reaches it from below in a single night.

Frost is the failure that ruins the job, not feet. Water in the mix freezes, expands about 9%, and pushes the finished surface off in flakes. You will not see it in November. You will see it in April, as a dusted, pitted walk that looks ten years old and cannot be repaired with a resurfacer because there is nothing solid left to bond to.

Two details make the difference between a walk that looks original and one that reads as a weekend project. Tool or saw a joint every 5 feet on a 3-foot-wide walk; the 2-to-3-times-thickness rule gives 8 to 12 feet as the maximum spacing for a 4-inch slab, and a narrow walk looks better with tighter panels anyway. Then broom the finish. A troweled walk is slick the first time it rains, and a November walk in a cold climate spends five months wet or icy.

What to buy ahead, what to skip

  • Form lumber and stakes. Two 2x4s ripped to 4 inches, staked every 4 feet, set to a 1/4 inch per foot fall away from the house. About $40 at current prices.
  • Compacted base. Four inches of 3/4-inch crushed stone, tamped. This is the part that prevents the heave you are trying to fix.
  • Edger and groover. A radius edger rounds the corner so it does not chip, and the groover cuts the joint before the surface sets.
  • Mesh or bar. A residential walk typically gets 6x6 welded wire mesh or #3 bar at 18 inches on center. The rebar calculator sizes either one for the length you are pouring.
  • 6-mil plastic or an insulating blanket. Twenty dollars of sheeting, weighted at the edges, is the entire cold-weather defense for a slab this size.
  • Non-chloride accelerator if the pour has to happen late. Calcium chloride works faster but raises shrinkage and can mottle the color, which matters on a walk you will look at every day.

Skip three things. Extra water to make the mix easier to place costs roughly 200 psi of strength per added gallon per yard, and that is how a walk ends up chalky by spring. Pouring over ground that is frozen solid, which guarantees a cracked panel by February. And any resurfacer or sealer applied to concrete younger than 28 days, which traps the mix's own moisture under a skin that then blisters.

Thanksgiving walkway questions

Can guests walk on concrete poured 3 days before Thanksgiving?

Yes. Foot traffic is fine at 24 to 48 hours when the concrete is held near 70°F, and a 3-day-old slab is around 40% of design strength, far more than a person in boots needs. Keep vehicles off for 7 days, and keep the hose off it entirely until the surface has cured.

Will one 30°F night ruin a new walk?

Only if the concrete itself freezes before it reaches about 500 psi, which takes one to two days at 50°F. Cover it with 6-mil plastic weighted down at the edges, or an insulated blanket, and keep the surface above freezing. One cold night on a covered slab is survivable. A cold night on an uncovered, saturated slab is how a surface dusts off under a broom the next morning.

Is it cheaper to pour now or wait until spring?

Material costs the same in April, and most plants price ready-mix within a few dollars a yard year-round. What changes is the risk: a spring pour has three weeks of mild weather behind it and no holiday deadline, so a rain delay costs you nothing. Winter concrete is the same number with a smaller margin for error.

The call

Pour on the 12th or 13th if the forecast holds above 40°F, order 0.81 yards, and cover it for the first two nights whether or not you think you need to. If the window has already closed and the ground is frozen, patch the two bad joints and leave the new slab for April. The walk costs the same then, and a slab that froze at 24 hours costs twice.

The trade-off is time against finish quality. Thirty-seven bags is $220 to $300 and a hard Saturday, and you will likely end up with a visible cold joint where batch three met batch four. A finisher at $1,100 gets a broom finish that matches the driveway and a slab poured in one continuous run. If the walk is visible from the street and you plan to stay in the house, that is the money well spent.
