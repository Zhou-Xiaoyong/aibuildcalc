How Much Concrete Do I Need for Fence Posts

Published August 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Undersizing your post holes is the fastest way to end up with a fence that leans after the first hard frost. The math is simple once you know two rules of thumb — the hole should be about three times the post width and about one-third of the post's above-ground height deep — and then it is just cylinder volume times your post count. This guide gives you the formula, a worked example for a standard 4x4 post, and a bag-count table so you can order with confidence.

Quick answer: A 4x4 fence post in a 12-inch-diameter hole 30 inches deep needs about 1.75 cubic feet of concrete — roughly 3 bags of 80 lb mix, 4 bags of 60 lb, or 5 bags of 50 lb per hole. Multiply by your post count with the Concrete Footing Calculator.

The hole-sizing rules of thumb

Before any math, settle the hole dimensions. Two rules keep almost every residential fence stable:

  • Diameter: make the hole about 3 times the post width. A nominal 4x4 post is actually 3.5 inches across, so a 10- to 12-inch hole is the sweet spot.
  • Depth: bury roughly one-third of the post's height above ground, and never less than your local frost depth. For a 6-foot fence, plan on 24-30 inches of buried post; in cold climates that can reach 36-48 inches.

A wider, deeper hole uses more concrete but resists uplift and rot far better. If you are setting a gate post or a corner, go one size larger than the run-of-the-mill line posts.

The per-hole volume formula

A post hole is a cylinder. The concrete fills the cylinder minus the space the post itself occupies:

Hole radius (ft) = Hole diameter (in) / 24 Hole volume (cu ft) = 3.1416 x Radius^2 x Depth (ft) Net concrete (cu ft) = Hole volume - (Post width ft x Post depth ft) Bag count (50 lb) = Net concrete / 0.375 Bag count (60 lb) = Net concrete / 0.45 Bag count (80 lb) = Net concrete / 0.60

Those divisors are the typical yields: a 50 lb bag makes about 0.375 cubic feet of cured concrete, a 60 lb bag about 0.45, and an 80 lb bag about 0.60. Yields vary slightly by mix, so check the bag.

Worked example: a standard 4x4 line post

Take a 3.5-inch post in a 12-inch hole, 30 inches (2.5 ft) deep:

Radius = 12 / 24 = 0.50 ft Hole volume = 3.1416 x 0.50^2 x 2.5 = 1.96 cu ft Post volume = (3.5/12) x (3.5/12) x 2.5 = 0.21 cu ft Net concrete = 1.96 - 0.21 = 1.75 cu ft Bags: 1.75 / 0.375 = 4.7 -> 5 x 50 lb, 4 x 60 lb, or 3 x 80 lb

So one typical line post takes about 1.75 cubic feet of mix. Most homeowners reach for 50 lb or 60 lb bags because they are easier to lug around the yard than 80 lb sacks.

Concrete for a whole run of fence

Multiply the per-post figure by the number of posts. A 6-foot wood privacy fence with posts every 8 feet over 100 feet needs about 13 posts. At 5 bags of 50 lb each, that is roughly 65 bags. The Concrete Footing Calculator handles irregular spacing automatically.

Bagged mix vs. a small ready-mix order

The break-even between bagged mix and a short ready-mix load is usually around 1 to 1.5 cubic yards. Under that, bags win on convenience: no pump, no cleanup window, and you can mix as you set each post. For most backyard fences (under about 150-200 linear feet) bags are the practical choice.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the post volume: forgetting to subtract the post overstates concrete by 10-15% on a narrow hole.
  • Shallow holes: setting posts only 18 inches deep in a freeze climate lets frost heave tip them within a season.
  • Wrong bag math: 50 lb and 80 lb bags do not have the same yield — do not assume "a bag is a bag."
  • No gravel tip: a 2-3 inch crushed-stone base under each post improves drainage and slows rot at the ground line.
  • Tamping dry: add water to the hole per the bag's ratio and tamp in lifts; pouring a dry bag into a wet hole never cures properly.
