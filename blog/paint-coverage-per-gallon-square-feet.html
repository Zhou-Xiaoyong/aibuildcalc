Paint Coverage Per Gallon Square Feet

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Every paint can carries a coverage figure, and almost every paint job uses more paint than that figure predicts. The label number is not wrong, it is conditional: it describes one coat on a smooth, sealed, previously painted surface under ideal conditions. Real walls are rarely any of those things, and the gap between the label and the wall is what turns a two-gallon estimate into a second trip to the store.

Quick answer: A gallon of interior paint covers about 350 to 400 square feet per coat on a smooth, primed or previously painted wall. Expect 200 to 300 square feet on bare drywall, new wood or heavy texture, and 150 to 250 square feet on brick or masonry. Work your own surfaces through the Paint Calculator.

The 350 to 400 square foot rule

Manufacturers print coverage on the label because it is a fair average for the most common case: a second coat over a sound existing coating. The figure assumes a single coat at the recommended wet film thickness, applied with a roller that is not overloaded and not thinned.

Gallons per coat = Surface area (sq ft) / Coverage per gallon Total gallons = Gallons per coat x Number of coats Example: 400 sq ft wall x 2 coats / 375 sq ft per gallon = 2.14 gallons Buy 3 gallons, because paint is sold in whole cans. Rooms: gal = Wall area x Coats / 375

Two mistakes are common when people use this rule. The first is applying the 375 square foot figure to every surface regardless of condition. The second is forgetting to multiply by the number of coats, and then being surprised when two gallons of wall paint will not cover a two-coat job on a 12 by 12 room.

Coverage by product type

Different products genuinely cover different areas, mostly because of solids content and how the film levels. The ranges below are typical label and real-world figures for a single coat on a prepared surface.

ProductCoverage per gallonTypical use
Interior flat or matte350-400 sq ftLow-traffic walls, ceilings
Interior eggshell350-400 sq ftLiving areas, hallways
Interior semi-gloss300-350 sq ftTrim, doors, kitchens, baths
Ceiling paint350-400 sq ftOverhead, splatter-resistant
Exterior acrylic250-350 sq ftSiding, trim, doors
Primer over bare drywall200-300 sq ftNew construction, patched walls
Primer over bare wood200-300 sq ftNew trim, doors, siding
Wood stain150-250 sq ftDecks, fences, siding

Trim paint covers less per gallon for a practical reason rather than a chemical one: brush work applies a thinner, more variable film than a roller, and much of the paint ends up in the tray and on the brush rather than on the door.

Why real coverage is lower than the label

  • Porosity. Bare drywall, joint compound and raw wood absorb paint on the first coat. Expect 200 to 300 square feet instead of 375.
  • Texture. Orange peel, knockdown and skip trowel increase the surface area of the wall itself, cutting coverage by 10 to 25 percent.
  • Color change. Going from a dark wall to a light one, or from a saturated red to an off-white, adds a hiding problem on top of a coverage problem.
  • Application method. Spraying is fast but the overspray becomes loss; brushing covers less area per gallon than rolling.
  • Thinning and cheap rollers. Thinning beyond the label instruction and using a thin-nap cover both lay down a lighter film.
  • Hot, dry, windy conditions. On exteriors, fast drying can flash and leave thin spots that need touch-up.

How many gallons a normal room needs

Using the standard estimate, a 12 by 12 foot room with 8 foot ceilings has a 48 foot perimeter and about 350 square feet of wall after openings. Two coats at 375 square feet per gallon comes to 1.87 gallons, so buy two. Include the ceiling and you add 144 square feet, about 0.77 of a gallon, which is why a small room plus ceiling plus a door and trim usually lands at three gallons of wall and ceiling paint plus a quart of trim enamel.

For a whole house, the arithmetic scales linearly: a 2,000 square foot home with average ceilings has roughly 6,000 square feet of wall and ceiling to cover, which is about 32 gallons per coat before windows and doors are subtracted. That is why contractors buy five-gallon pails and why the per-gallon price is worth comparing at both sizes.

Coats, recoat windows and the second-coat question

Two coats are standard for any color change and for anything approaching a finished surface, because a single coat over a different color will telegraph the old tone through in grazing light. Latex paint is usually recoatable in two to four hours and oil-based trim paint in six to eight. The second coat is also the cheapest coat you will ever apply in terms of time, because the masking and cleanup are already done.

Buying the paint

Round up to whole cans and keep a labeled quart of each color for touch-ups; dye lots vary between batches, so a later purchase may not match.
