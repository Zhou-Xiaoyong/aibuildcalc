Paint Cost Per Square Foot Interior

Published September 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Interior painting is usually quoted by the square foot because that is the one number a homeowner can check against a floor plan. The catch is that two different square footages get used for the same job, the floor area of the room and the actual wall surface, and the two differ by a factor of roughly two and a half. Knowing which one a bid is using is the difference between a fair price and an expensive misunderstanding.

Quick answer: Most US painters in 2026 charge $1.50 to $3.50 per square foot of floor area for a standard two-coat interior repaint, which works out to roughly $0.60 to $1.50 per square foot of actual wall surface. Material alone is about $0.20 to $0.50 per square foot of wall; the rest is labor. Build your own number with the Painting Cost Calculator.

Floor area or wall area: which one is the price based on?

Both conventions are in use, which is why two quotes for the same room can look wildly different. Floor-area pricing is the common shorthand among contractors because it can be estimated without measuring every ceiling and closet. Wall-area pricing is more precise. The relationship between them is predictable enough to convert on the spot.

Wall area (sq ft) ≈ Floor area (sq ft) x 2.5 to 3.0 (for a typical 8 ft ceiling with one door and two windows per room) Cost = Wall area x (Paint and supplies per sq ft + Labor per sq ft) Or, by floor area: Cost = Floor area x bundled rate per sq ft

A 12x12 room has 144 square feet of floor and about 333 square feet of wall after openings, a multiplier near 2.3. Large open rooms trend lower because there is less trim and corner cutting per square foot; small rooms with heavy trim trend higher. Any bid that names a per-square-foot rate should also state which square footage it applies to.

The labor and material split

On a typical interior repaint, labor runs 70 to 80 percent of the total and materials 20 to 30 percent. That ratio explains why the room's condition moves the price far more than the paint brand does.

Paint, walls, two coats, mid-grade$0.20-$0.40 per sq ft of wall15-20%
Primer and patching compound$0.05-$0.15 per sq ft of wall3-6%

Labor: prep, masking, setup$0.25-$0.60 per sq ft of wall20-30%
Labor: cutting in and rolling$0.35-$0.90 per sq ft of wall45-55%

Premium paint adds real money to the material line but shortens the labor line, because a higher solids product often covers in two coats where a builder-grade paint needs three. On a whole-house repaint the two effects can roughly cancel, which is why experienced painters still push mid-grade paint for rentals and premium for owner-occupied homes.

Cost per square foot by surface

Walls are the cheapest surface per square foot because a roller covers ground quickly. Ceilings cost more because overhead work is slower and fatiguing. Trim costs the most because every inch is brushed by hand.

What pushes a price above the average

  • Ceiling height. Anything over 9 feet needs taller ladders or scaffolding and slows the crew by 15 to 30 percent.
  • Prep and repair. Patching nail pops, skim-coating damaged drywall, and sanding glossy trim can add $0.30 to $1.00 per square foot of wall on its own.
  • Number of colors. Each additional wall color adds cutting-in time; accent walls and stripes are quoted as extras.
  • Occupied home. Moving and protecting furniture, plus working around a family, typically adds 10 to 20 percent.
  • Color change. Going from a dark saturated color to a light one generally needs a tinted primer or a third coat.

DIY versus hiring

Doing the work yourself removes the labor line, which is the majority of the cost, and leaves materials at roughly $0.30 to $0.60 per square foot of wall including tape and rollers. On a 1,500 square foot home that is a few hundred dollars of paint against a professional quote in the several thousands, which is why interior painting remains one of the most common DIY projects. The trade is time, ladder work, and the risk of a visible lap line or a dripped trim edge, which is exactly what the labor portion of a pro bid buys. If the walls are tall, the ceilings are vaulted, or the finish has to be flawless before a sale, the premium price is usually worth paying.

Getting a number you can trust

Ask any painter to state whether the rate is per square foot of floor or of wall, how many coats are included, and whether trim and ceilings are in scope. Three good bids on the same stated scope beat one low bid on a vague one.
