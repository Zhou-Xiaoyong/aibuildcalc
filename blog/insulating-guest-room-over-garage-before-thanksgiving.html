Guest Room Over the Garage: How Much Insulation Before Thanksgiving? 19 Bags, $600

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 20 by 22 bonus room over a garage is 440 square feet with 269 square feet of exposed wall, and on a 28°F night it loses about 4,310 BTU an hour. The single 6-inch register feeding it delivers 3,240. No thermostat setting closes that gap. Nineteen bags of insulation and one Saturday of air sealing bring the loss down to 2,610 BTU an hour for $600 in material, which is the difference between a room guests use and a room they avoid.

Quick answer: R-49 in the ceiling, R-13 in the two exterior walls, and every gap at the top plate and rim sealed. For this room that is 4 bags of R-13 batts and 15 bags of blown cellulose, about $600 in material, 9 to 11 hours of work. Start by November 22. If you only get one weekend, blow the ceiling and air-seal; the wall batts can wait until spring. Size your own room in the Insulation Calculator before you buy anything.

The takeoff starts at the ceiling, not the floor

A paint estimate begins with wall area. An insulation estimate begins with every surface that touches outside air, and in a bonus room that is three surfaces plus the leaks between them.

The ceiling sits under an unconditioned attic and takes the biggest temperature hit of anything in the room. The two gable-end walls are the only walls exposed to weather; the other two back onto attic space over the main house and need nothing. The floor over the garage is the surface everyone wants to fix and the one that cannot be fixed in a weekend, because improving it means opening the garage ceiling from below. Houses built in the late 1990s usually have R-19 batts in those joists, and R-19 is what you live with.

Measure gross and deduct the openings. A 3 by 7 door is 21 square feet and two 3 by 5 windows are 30 more, so 320 square feet of gross wall becomes 269 net.

This is the part that makes the case for spending a weekend. Heat loss and heat supply are both in BTU per hour, so they subtract directly, and the smaller number wins.

The supply is fixed at roughly 3,240 BTU an hour, which is what a 6-inch boot at 100 cfm and a 30-degree temperature rise will carry. Below 4,310 it cannot hold the room, so the room drops until the loss falls to match. That balance lands near 60 degrees with the thermostat at 70 everywhere else in the house. After the work the loss is 2,610, the register has 630 BTU an hour of headroom, and the room holds setpoint. Run your own areas through the BTU Calculator if the room is not 20 by 22.

Attic temperature is why the ceiling dominates. An attic at 30°F on a 28°F night is close to outdoor air, while the garage stays near 45 because the house shares a wall with it. Same room, wildly different loads per square foot.

The ceiling and the walls want different products, which is why the shopping list has two kinds of insulation on it rather than one.

R-49 is the 2021 IECC ceiling target for climate zones 4 through 8, and almost every state north of Tennessee has adopted it in some form. Check the R-Value Calculator for your zone before you decide the ceiling is done at R-38. Blowing to R-38 because that is what the bag says on the side is the most common miss on this job.

Air sealing is the cheapest third of the job

Look at the table again. Air leakage is 1,512 BTU an hour, more than the ceiling, and $42 of foam and caulk cuts it roughly in half. Nothing else on the list has that ratio.

Four places matter in a bonus room. The top plate where the wall meets the attic floor, which is usually wide open on the garage side. The rim joist at the band, which is a straight shot from the attic into the wall cavity. The supply boot, cut through drywall with a half-inch gap all the way around. And any recessed can that is not IC-rated, because those cannot be buried in cellulose at all.

Two rules while you are up there. Keep the rafter baffles clear at the eaves so the soffit vents still breathe, and never cover knob-and-tube wiring with loose fill. If the house is from 1998, the cans are almost certainly IC-rated and stamped on the trim.

Thanksgiving insulation questions

Can I blow cellulose over the old fiberglass batts?

Yes, and it is the normal way to get from R-13 to R-49 without pulling anything out. The batts stay in the joist bays and the cellulose fills the 9.9 inches above them, which also covers the top plates and the gaps between bays. Do not compress the old batts to make room. Compressed fiberglass loses R-value in almost direct proportion to its thickness.

Does the wall need a vapor barrier in Zone 5?

Kraft facing on the batts, installed with the paper toward the heated room, is the code-accepted approach for a 2x4 wall in this climate, and it doubles as the air barrier if you staple and seal the flanges. A polyethylene sheet behind drywall is not required and can trap moisture if the wall is not detailed carefully. Seal the flanges to the studs with adhesive caulk rather than relying on the staples alone.

Is R-49 overkill for a room used four nights a year?

The energy math says yes and the comfort math says no. Sixty therms a season is $77, so $600 of material takes about eight years to pay back in gas. What you are actually buying is a room that holds 68 degrees while twelve people are in the house, and that has nothing to do with the gas bill. If the room is truly a spare, seal the air leaks, add a $70 oil-filled radiator, and put the money into the guest bath instead. The guest bedroom conversion costs are the other half of that decision.

The call

Blow the ceiling and air-seal this weekend. That is 15 bags, $42 of foam and caulk, and six hours, and it removes 1,900 of the 4,310 BTU an hour the room is losing. Add the wall batts next spring when the attic is warm and the blower is not competing with everyone else in the county.

The trade-off is honest. You will spend a Saturday in a fiberglass mask for a room that returns $77 a season, and the floor over the garage stays at R-19 no matter what you do, so the carpet will still read cold underfoot in February. What changes is that the register stops losing the fight. Guests sleep at 68 instead of 60, and nobody asks for an extra blanket at dinner. If the room only ever holds a folding table and a box of decorations, skip all of it and put the $600 toward the furnace tune-up that the heating cost breakdown says will return more.
