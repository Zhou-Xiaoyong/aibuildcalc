Cyber Monday Wet Saw Deal: How Many Rental Days Before Buying Pays Off? Five

Published November 2026 · 7 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A $349 wet saw at 20 percent off comes to $279, and a porcelain blade adds $45, so $324 owns the tool outright. The rental counter wants $68 a day for the same 7-inch saw. Divide one by the other and the break-even is 4.8 days, or about five. A single 200 sq ft bathroom needs two cutting days, so the Cyber Monday cart loses to the rental desk on the first job and only wins on the second.

Quick answer: add up everything you would own, saw plus blade plus stand, and divide by the daily rental rate. On a benchtop saw that is $324 over $68, so five days. The number that actually decides the purchase is square footage, not the discount: five cutting days at a homeowner's pace is 400 to 450 sq ft of 12x24 tile, which is one large bathroom plus a hallway or a second bath. Run your own area through the Tile Calculator before you check out, because a shower pan and a diagonal lay add 10 to 15 percent to both the tile order and the cutting time.

Cyber Monday closes Monday night. The rental desk opens Saturday morning.

Two clocks are running against each other this weekend. Benchtop tile saws typically sit 20 to 25 percent below list through Monday night and go back to list on Tuesday, which is the only reason this decision is on a deadline at all. The rental counter bills in 24-hour blocks instead, and a machine picked up Saturday morning is due back Sunday morning unless the store sells a weekend rate.

Ask about that weekend rate before you reserve. Some counters quote a flat Saturday-to-Monday price that works out to less than one day; others bill the full three. If yours bills all three, the rental side of this math nearly doubles and the answer flips toward buying for almost any job.

What the discount cannot do is change the size of your floor. Twenty percent off is $70 on this saw. A second rental day is $68. One extra day of cutting erases the entire Cyber Monday saving, which is why the deadline should not be the thing that decides it.

The five-day number, line by line

Break-even is total cost of ownership divided by the daily rental rate. Nothing in the arithmetic is subtle, but almost every version of this comparison leaves out the blade and the stand, and those are a third of the outlay.

The stand is the line people skip. A benchtop saw weighs 50 to 70 lb and most of them are set up on a bucket in a driveway, which is how a thumb ends up under a moving table. A folding stand is $70 to $120, or two sawhorses you already own at the right height. Add the stand and the break-even moves from 4.8 days to 6.2.

Why the $279 saw cannot cut the tile people are buying

Most benchtop 7-inch saws have a table about 14 inches deep and a blade guard that stops the work at roughly 12 inches. That handles 12x12, subway tile and mosaic sheets without complaint. It does not handle 12x24, the size that has taken over bathroom floors: the 24-inch length will not pass under the guard, and ripping it lengthwise means flipping the tile, which leaves the two kerfs a sixteenth apart and chips the glaze where the blade exits.

You can finish those cuts with an angle grinder and a straightedge. You will also see every one of them, because a grinder cut in porcelain is a ragged line unless you spend two minutes on each pass. On a shower wall that is the difference between a job you are proud of and a job you cover with a trim piece.

A 10-inch sliding saw cross-cuts 24 to 30 inches and costs close to twice as much. That is the saw large-format tile requires, and it moves the break-even from five days to seven. Both numbers are in the table below, because the honest answer depends on which tile is stacked in your garage.

Above 400 sq ft, owning wins on money before it ever wins on convenience, and the tool is still in the garage for the next floor.

Zoom out and the saw is the small number. At $7 to $14 a sq ft for labor, 200 sq ft of porcelain runs $1,400 to $2,800 installed, which the installed cost breakdown lays out line by line. A $324 saw is under a quarter of the labor on the smallest job in that table. The choice was never between two saws. It is between a saw and paying someone else to do the cutting, and the Cyber Monday discount is a rounding error in that comparison.

What has to be in the cart before the $279 is real

  • Blade. Forty to ninety dollars for porcelain. A $40 blade cuts 500 to 1,000 sq ft before it glazes over; a $15 blade will not finish the first bathroom on hard porcelain, and that is usually the moment somebody decides the saw is bad.
  • Stand or two sawhorses. Seventy to a hundred twenty dollars, at a height where the table sits near your waist. Cutting on the floor bends the back and puts your hands in the wrong place.
  • Leveling clips. Ten to twenty cents a sq ft. Lippage between two 12x24 tiles is the defect you cannot fix after the thin-set cures, and it shows up worst under a raking window light.
  • Floor prep. A flat floor matters more than the saw. Three-eighths of an inch out of level over 10 ft will telegraph through large tile no matter how clean the cuts are, and the leveling sequence costs a bag of patching compound instead of a tool.

Two habits cost nothing and matter as much. Back-butter the tile: ANSI A108.5 wants 95 percent mortar contact under porcelain in a wet area, and a trowel alone rarely gets there on a 12x24. And buy the tile before the saw, because the box count with waste determines how many cutting days you are actually signing up for.

If Monday night passes and you did not buy

The price comes back. Benchtop tile saws land within 5 to 10 percent of late-November pricing in the first two weeks of January, and the spread on this saw is about $30. That is not a reason to make a $324 decision on a Monday deadline.

Used is the honest middle path. Seven-inch saws turn up for $120 to $180, and the blade on them is usually shot, so budget $45 the same week. A used saw with a new blade is a $200 machine that cuts the same tile as the $279 one.

Renting twice is also allowed. Two bathroom rentals are $272 and still cheaper than owning, and by the third one you know whether tile is a hobby or a one-time repair. What you should not do is buy the 7-inch saw in November and then discover in February that the next floor is 12x24.

Cyber Monday tile saw questions

Is a wet saw worth buying for a single bathroom?

No. A 200 sq ft bathroom needs about two cutting days, which rents for $136 against $324 to own, and a benchtop saw takes up a shelf for the other 363 days. Buy it when the second job is already scheduled, not when it is imagined.

Can an angle grinder replace a wet saw?

For notches, L-cuts and door casings, yes, and most professionals reach for one there. For the field of a shower floor or a long rip down a 24-inch plank, no: the cut wanders, the glaze chips, and the dust goes everywhere without water. Budget a grinder as a $90 supplement, not a $324 substitute.

Do tile saws get cheaper after Cyber Monday?

Marginally. The late-December clearance and the first weeks of January usually land 5 to 10 percent apart from the Cyber Monday price, in either direction. If the job is not on the calendar, waiting costs almost nothing and buys you a month to measure the floor properly.

The call

One bathroom, rent it. Sixty-eight dollars on Saturday morning, back before the counter closes on Sunday, and the $324 stays in the account. Four hundred square feet or more, or a second bath on next year's list, buy the 10-inch tonight at $599 all in, because seven rental days cost $602 and the saw stays in your garage.

The trade-off is a 60 lb machine that sits eleven months a year, a blade that glazes if you store it wet, and a stand that takes up the corner where the ladder goes. Most garages already have one of these saws, and most of them were bought for a job that a $68 rental would have finished.
