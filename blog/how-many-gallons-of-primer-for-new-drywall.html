How Many Gallons of Primer for New Drywall?

Published September 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

New drywall is the thirstiest surface in a house. Joint compound and the paper facing of the board absorb paint at wildly different rates, and if you skip primer you will see every seam through the topcoat no matter how many coats you apply. The good news is that primer is cheap and the coverage arithmetic is simple.

Quick answer: On bare new drywall, one gallon of primer covers only about 200 to 300 square feet, roughly half of what it covers on a previously painted wall. A typical 12x12 room with 8 foot ceilings needs about 1 gallon for the walls; a 1,500 square foot home needs roughly 5 to 7 gallons of primer for walls and ceilings together. Why bare drywall eats primer

Two surfaces are being primed at once, and they behave differently. The paper facing of gypsum board is porous and pulls primer in quickly. Joint compound is even more absorbent and, because it is applied as a wet mud and sanded, it has a different texture and sheen than the paper around it. A topcoat applied straight to that mix soaks in unevenly, and the difference shows up as flashing, the patchy "picture frame" look around every seam and corner. Primer evens out the absorption so the finish coat can do its job.

The coverage numbers that matter

Gallons per coat = Paintable area (sq ft) / Coverage (sq ft per gallon) Total primer = Gallons per coat x Coats, then round up to whole containers Paintable area = walls (perimeter x height - openings) + ceiling (length x width)

Coverage is the number people get wrong, because the can often lists a best-case figure measured on a sealed surface. The table below reflects what primer actually covers on the surfaces you meet in a real job.

Surface being primedCoverage per gallonNotes
Bare new drywall, first coat200-300 sq ftWorst case; the board and the mud both drink it in
Bare drywall, second coat350-450 sq ftThe first coat sealed the surface
Previously painted wall400-450 sq ftOnly needed for color changes or repairs
Sanded joint compound only150-250 sq ftSpot-priming patches; the driest surface of all
Sealed or skim-coated masonry250-350 sq ftVaries widely with texture

How many gallons for a whole house

For new construction, painters typically estimate primer from the same wall-and-ceiling area used for paint, then divide by a lower coverage figure. The benchmarks below assume bare board, one primer coat, and average ceilings.

On large jobs primer is cheaper by the five-gallon pail than by the single gallon, and buying a pail or two extra costs far less than stopping mid-wall to fetch more. Keep a gallon sealed for touch-ups after the trim is installed.

One coat or two?

One coat is standard and usually sufficient when the primer is a quality drywall primer and the walls are to be finished in a mid-to-light color. Add a second primer coat, or switch to a heavier product, in four situations: the finish color is deep and saturated, the primer appears blotchy after it dries, the drywall saw heavy sanding that left a dusty, uneven surface, or you are painting over a repaired area where new compound meets old paint. In those cases the second coat is cheaper than the extra finish coat it prevents.

For plain new drywall with no staining, PVA is the right answer and spending more buys little. Reach for a sealing primer only when there is something to seal.

Preparation matters as much as the primer

  • Dust the walls. Sanding dust left on the board clogs the primer and shows as grit in the finish. Wipe or vacuum before priming.
  • Check for pinholes and ridges. Primer does not hide flaws; it locks them in. Inspect under a bright light first.
  • Prime before caulking trim. Primer on the wall, then caulk the gap, then paint, in that order.
  • Do not thin the primer. Adding water to stretch coverage defeats the sealing the wall needs.

The bottom line

Buy primer by the true coverage figure, not the label: 200 to 300 square feet per gallon on bare board, one coat for most rooms, two where the finish is dark or the surface is uneven. It is the least expensive step in the whole paint job and the one that decides how the finished walls look.
