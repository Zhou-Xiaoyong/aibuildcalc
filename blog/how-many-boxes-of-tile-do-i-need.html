How Many Boxes of Tile Do I Need

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Tile is sold by the box, priced by the square foot and installed by the piece, which is why the quantity question trips up so many first-time projects. The number printed on the carton is the only reliable figure, and it changes with tile size, thickness and manufacturer. The method below gets you to a defensible box count before you order, so you are not making a second trip mid-install with a different dye lot.

Quick answer: Measure the area in square feet, multiply by 1.10 for a straight lay or 1.15 for a diagonal lay, then divide by the square feet per box printed on the carton and round up. A 144 sq ft room in 12 by 24 tile at 16 sq ft per box needs 158.4 / 16 = 9.9, so 10 boxes. The Tile Calculator does this with your own dimensions and waste percentage.

Step one: measure the area you are actually tiling

Work in feet and inches and convert to decimal feet before multiplying. For a rectangular room that is length times width. For an L-shaped room, split it into two rectangles and add them. For a room with a permanent island or a built-in cabinet run, measure the open floor and add the tile that will run under the appliance gaps separately, because installers still cut around them.

Do not use the room label from a floor plan. A "12 by 12 bedroom" is often 11 foot 8 inches by 12 foot 3 inches once drywall and trim are in, and that small difference changes the box count at the margin.

Step two: read the square feet per box, not the piece count

Cartons vary widely, and the same nominal tile size can ship in different quantities between brands. The table below shows the typical range for common formats in the US market. Treat these as approximate and always confirm against the carton you are buying.

Tile formatPieces per box (typical)Square feet per box
12 x 12 in10 to 1510 to 15 sq ft
12 x 24 in6 to 812 to 16 sq ft
18 x 18 in5 to 611 to 13.5 sq ft
24 x 24 in416 sq ft

Step three: add waste for the layout

Waste is not a cushion for bad measuring, it is the material consumed by cuts at walls, around doorways and inside corners. Straight lay in a simple rectangular room needs about 10 percent. A diagonal or 45-degree lay needs 15 percent because every perimeter cut produces an offcut that cannot be reused. Herringbone and chevron patterns routinely waste 20 percent or more. Small rooms with many obstacles sit at the top of each range.

Boxes = (area sq ft x (1 + waste %)) / sq ft per box Round the result up to the next whole box. Example: 144 sq ft, straight lay, 12 x 24 tile at 16 sq ft per box 144 x 1.10 = 158.4 sq ft 158.4 / 16 = 9.9 Order 10 boxes (160 sq ft)

What the box does not include

Tile is only one line of the material list. Thinset covers roughly 50 to 95 square feet per 50 lb bag depending on trowel size, so a 144 sq ft floor typically needs two to three bags. Grout is calculated from tile size, joint width and thickness rather than from floor area, and a 1/8 inch joint in large-format tile uses noticeably less than a 3/8 inch joint in small mosaic. Add backer board or uncoupling membrane, screws, spacers, edge trim and a wet saw blade if you do not own one.

Ordering tips that save a second trip

Buy the whole job in one purchase so every box shares a dye lot and calibre rating; tile from a different batch can be visibly different in the same room. Keep one unopened box as attic stock for future repairs, and check the return policy before you open anything, because many retailers only accept full unopened cartons. If the calculated count lands exactly on a whole box with no margin, buy one extra rather than gambling on breakage.
