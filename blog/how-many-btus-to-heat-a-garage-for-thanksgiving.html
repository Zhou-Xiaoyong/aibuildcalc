How Many BTUs to Heat a 400-Sq-Ft Garage for Thanksgiving? 22,000

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 20 by 20 attached garage at 28 degrees outside needs 19,000 BTU an hour to hold 68 degrees inside, and 22,000 with the usual pickup allowance. That is a 5,000-watt electric heater with a 240-volt circuit behind it, or a 30,000-BTU propane unit turned down to half. One 1,500-watt plug-in covers 27 percent of it, which balances the room at 39 degrees.

Quick answer: a two-car garage with no insulation sits near 47 BTU per square foot in late November, which is where the 19,000 comes from. The overhead door is a tenth of the surface area and a quarter of the load, so $120 of door seal and foil-faced panel cuts 6,000 BTU before you buy a heater. For Thursday, plan on $200 to $450 for electric or $180 to $350 for propane, plus $8 to $12 to run either one for the day. The BTU Calculator does this audit on your own dimensions.

A garage is a box with two bad walls and a very bad door

Numbers first. Twenty by twenty, nine-foot ceiling, one 16 by 7 overhead door, one 36-inch man door into the kitchen. That is 587 square feet of wall, 400 of ceiling, 112 of door, and 80 linear feet of slab edge sitting on grade.

Uninsulated, the assembly lands where you would guess. Six-inch frame walls with siding and half-inch sheathing run about R-4. A drywall ceiling under a vented attic is R-3. A single-layer steel overhead door is the outlier at R-1, and it is also where the air gets in, because a sixteen-foot door has sixteen feet of bottom seal, two vertical jambs and four hinges to leak through. Budget 1.5 air changes an hour on a box that size, and that is generous.

Plug those into the standard load equation and the total is 18,790 BTU an hour. Add 15 percent for pickup, the margin a heater needs to recover after a door opens, and the number to shop with is 22,000.

The math, surface by surface

Heat loss is conductance times area times temperature difference, which is why a 112-square-foot door at R-1 can outrun 587 square feet of wall at R-4. The temperature difference here is 40 degrees, a 28-degree afternoon and a 68-degree room.

Q = U x A x dT, where U = 1 / R Design conditions Outdoor 28 F, indoor 68 F dT = 40 F Garage 20 x 20 x 9 ft 400 sq ft floor, 1,800 cu ft Walls, R-4, U 0.22 (80 ft perimeter x 9) - 112 - 21 = 587 sq ft 587 x 0.22 x 40 = 5,166 BTU/h Ceiling, R-3, U 0.35 400 x 0.35 x 40 = 5,600 BTU/h Overhead door, R-1, U 1.10 112 x 1.10 x 40 = 4,928 BTU/h Slab edge, 80 linear ft 80 x 0.8 x (68 - 50) = 1,152 BTU/h Infiltration, 1.5 ACH 1,800 x 1.5 / 60 = 45 cfm 45 x 1.08 x 40 = 1,944 BTU/h Total = 18,790 BTU/h With 15% pickup = 21,608 -> 22,000 BTU/h Per square foot = 47 BTU/sq ft

Now the same garage with R-13 walls, R-30 ceiling, an R-6.5 door and half the air leakage: 4,808 BTU an hour, or 6,000 with pickup. Fifteen BTU per square foot instead of 47. That is the whole argument for insulation in two lines, and it is also why the 45-BTU-per-square-foot rule of thumb that gets passed around for garages oversizes an insulated one by a factor of three.

Fix the door before you buy the heater

A foil-faced foam kit for a 16 by 7 door runs $70 to $130 and pushes the door from R-1 to about R-6.5. A new bottom seal is $25 to $45 and a jamb weatherstrip kit another $20. Total outlay lands between $95 and $175, and it strips about 4,900 BTU of raw load, 6,000 once the pickup allowance is counted.

The wall and ceiling numbers assume batts in open stud bays, which is a different job on a finished garage. If the walls are already drywalled, the ceiling is the only cheap one left, and blown cellulose above the lid beats nothing by 5,600 BTU an hour for $400 to $600. Two things not to do this week. Do not insulate a wall that has a known moisture problem, and do not stuff batts against a garage door track; the door needs its travel. The guest room over the garage is the other half of this problem, and it is a bigger job than the garage itself.

What each heater actually delivers

Output is the only spec that matters on Thursday. A 1,500-watt plug-in is 5,118 BTU, no matter what the box calls it, and there is no such thing as a heater that makes more heat than the watts it draws. Electricity converts at 3,412 BTU per kWh; propane at roughly 91,500 BTU per gallon burned.

Run cost uses 17 cents a kWh, propane at $3 a gallon in a 20-pound exchange cylinder, and kerosene at $4.75. Your delivered propane price is the number that decides the propane row, and it swings from $2.40 to $4.10 depending on how much you buy at once.

The 5 kW electric unit is the clean answer if the wiring is there, and 17,060 BTU is enough to hold 64 degrees on its own. With the door kit in, it cycles at 68. Twenty-one amps against a 30-amp breaker at the 80 percent continuous limit is 24 usable, so the circuit has room and the heater runs dry. Look for a 30-amp dryer or welder receptacle on the garage wall before you pay an electrician; plenty of houses have one behind a shelf. If the panel is full, the Thanksgiving circuit load math covers what an electrician will actually charge to add one. The Wattage Calculator converts any nameplate into BTU before you buy.

Two plug-in heaters are the honest fallback and they must sit on separate circuits. Two 1,500-watt units on one 15-amp circuit draw 25 amps, which is a trip in ten minutes. On separate circuits they deliver 10,240 BTU, which is 54 percent of the design load, so the room balances at 50 degrees. Insulate the door and the same two heaters hold 57. Cool, workable, and a long way from 39.

The slab is why you start Wednesday

A 400-square-foot slab at four inches thick weighs about 20,000 pounds. Concrete holds 0.2 BTU per pound per degree, so lifting that slab from 50 to 70 degrees takes 80,000 BTU before a single degree of air temperature sticks. A 30,000-BTU heater running against a 19,000-BTU load has 11,000 BTU an hour of surplus, which means seven hours of running to bring the floor up.

The air is different. A garage that size turns over in 40 minutes with the heater on, and the room will read 65 on a thermometer while the floor still pulls heat out of anyone sitting at a folding table. Start Wednesday evening at 60 degrees, hold it overnight, and go to 68 Thursday morning. Guests who arrive at noon will not know the difference. Guests who arrive at noon into a cold garage will keep their coats on through the pie.

The 48-hour sequence

  • Today. Measure the door, order the foil-faced kit and a bottom seal, and buy two carbon monoxide alarms at $25 to $40 each. Call the propane dealer if you are going that route; refills and cylinder exchanges both slow down the week of Thanksgiving.
  • Tuesday morning. Pull the old bottom seal, clean the aluminum retainer, and slide the new one in. Foil-faced panels cut with a utility knife and press into the door's stamped recesses. Three hours for a 16 by 7 door, and it is the best money on this list.
  • Tuesday afternoon. Clear the floor. A garage full of strollers and lumber has half the usable volume and twice the fire load. Anything with a gas engine comes out, including the mower, before a heater goes in.
  • Wednesday, 6 p.m. Set the heater to 60, close the overhead door, and plug in a CO alarm inside the garage. Do not seal the room airtight; the man door and the eaves provide the air a vent-free unit needs, and a room with no air change is its own problem.
  • Thursday, 9 a.m. Raise the setpoint to 68. Put the second alarm in the hallway outside the bedrooms, which is where the code wants it anyway. Check the floor with a bare hand before you set the table.

Where the carbon monoxide comes from

An unvented propane heater burning 2.6 gallons puts about 2.3 gallons of water vapor into the room, because propane yields roughly 0.88 gallons of water per gallon burned. That water lands on a 50-degree slab and on every cold steel surface in the room. Tools rust, the floor gets slick, and the windows fog from the inside. It is also why a vented unit or electric heat is the better answer for a space people will sit in for six hours.

Carbon monoxide is the part that kills people, and the garage is the classic location. A house with an attached garage is required to have CO alarms under the International Residential Code, section R315, and the alarm belongs outside the sleeping rooms, not next to the furnace. Add one in the garage for the weekend. It costs $30 and it is the only instrument that will tell you the burner is running rich.

Three rules hold regardless of what you buy. Never idle a car in an attached garage, even with the overhead door open, because the door between the garage and the house is not airtight and CO goes where the air goes. Never run a charcoal grill, a generator or a propane turkey fryer indoors or in a garage, and keep the fryer ten feet off the siding when it is outside. And keep the house door closed and self-closing; the code wants a solid or 20-minute door there for fire separation, and propping it open for foot traffic throws away both the fire barrier and the air seal.

Thanksgiving garage heat questions

Will a 1,500-watt space heater heat a two-car garage?

Not on its own. It delivers 5,118 BTU against a 19,000 BTU load, so the room balances at 39 degrees with 28 outside and the element never shuts off. Two on separate circuits reach 50, and 57 once the overhead door is insulated. That last number is the difference between coats on and coats off.

Is a vent-free propane heater safe in a garage?

With a working CO alarm and air to breathe, it is common and it works. It is not the best choice for a room full of people, because it adds water and it puts combustion products into the space you are sitting in. A direct-vent unit or electric heat avoids both, and neither one needs a cylinder exchange on the day before a holiday.

How fast can I warm a cold garage?

The air, in under an hour. The slab, in six to eight hours, because 20,000 pounds of concrete is the largest heat sink in the building and it has to come up before the room feels warm at table height. That gap is the whole reason the plan starts Wednesday instead of Thursday morning.

The call

Buy the door kit and the seals today, $120, and put a 5,000-watt electric heater on the wall if a 30-amp circuit already exists. That combination holds 68 degrees for about $10 of electricity across the whole weekend, with no water vapor and no cylinder to fetch.

The trade-off is the circuit. Without one, the electric route means an electrician at $400 to $900 on a holiday week, which is not happening by Thursday. Propane is the answer in that case: $240 for a 30,000-BTU unit, $8 of fuel for the day, and 2.3 gallons of water on a cold slab that you mop up Friday. Take the propane and the door kit, skip the torpedo heater, and start warming the floor Wednesday night.
