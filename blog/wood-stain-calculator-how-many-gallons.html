Wood Stain Calculator How Many Gallons

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Stain coverage is quoted per gallon, but the gallon label describes a best case that few real decks ever match. Weathered cedar, wet pressure-treated lumber and rough-sawn fence pickets all drink far more product than the number on the can, and because stain cannot be matched across batches the way paint can, running short mid-project is a genuine problem.

Quick answer: A 12 by 16 foot deck with about 400 square feet of board area takes roughly 1.5 to 2.5 gallons per coat on smooth wood and 2.5 to 4 gallons per coat on rough or weathered boards. A deck is measured in board area, not footprint

A deck's footprint is only part of the story. A 12 by 16 foot deck is 192 square feet of floor plan, but the boards have two faces, a gap between them and edges that need coating so water cannot wick in from the side. In practice, multiply the footprint by about 2.1 for a surface-only job, and by about 3.0 if you are also staining the railings, the fascia and the stair stringers. That turns a 192 square foot deck into roughly 400 square feet of surface, or close to 580 including rails.

Deck surface (sq ft) = footprint (sq ft) x 2.1 (boards, top and sides) Deck plus rails (sq ft) = footprint (sq ft) x 3.0 Fence pickets (sq ft) = length (ft) x picket height (ft) x 1.7 Gallons = total area / coverage per gallon x number of coats

A fence is priced in linear feet

Fence pickets are cheap individually and expensive collectively, because every picket has two faces and two edges. A 100 foot privacy fence with 6 foot pickets exposes roughly 1,020 square feet once both faces and the gaps between pickets are counted, which at 150 square feet per gallon is close to 7 gallons per coat. Railings, posts and the top cap usually add 15 to 20 percent on top of that figure.

One coat or two?

A single coat of a transparent or semi-transparent stain will hold up for a season or two on a deck that already has some colour, and it is the standard maintenance routine for a board that was sealed a year ago. Bare or stripped wood needs two coats, because the first coat is absorbed rather than deposited and it leaves dry, starved patches behind. Solid-colour stains behave like thin paint and should always get two coats for even colour, especially where sun exposure differs across the deck.

Testing the wood before you buy

Spill a few ounces of water on the deck and watch how fast it disappears. If the water beads and sits for several minutes, the wood is still sealed and a single maintenance coat will sit on the surface rather than soak in, so you can plan on the higher end of the coverage range. If it darkens and vanishes within a minute, the grain is open and thirsty, and you should plan on the lower coverage figure plus a second coat. This thirty-second test is more reliable than any table, including the one above, because coverage is a property of the specific board in front of you rather than of the species on the label.

Spraying wastes more than it looks like

Sprayers are fast on pickets and railings but typically waste 15 to 20 percent of the product as overspray, and a breeze on an open deck can push that higher. Brush and roller application on flat decking, then a sprayer or a brush for the rails, is the standard compromise: full control where it matters, speed where the surface is repetitive. Whatever tool you use, add one spare quart to the total and keep it sealed, because a mid-job top-up from a different batch is the fastest way to end up with a visible patch.
