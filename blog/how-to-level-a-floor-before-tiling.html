How to Level a Floor Before Tiling

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 120 sq ft bathroom in a 1978 colonial drops 1/2 in from the door to the tub. A 12x24 porcelain tile laid over that plane rocks on the high corner and cracks grout within a season. Most homeowners price the fix by guessing at bags.

Quick answer: Leveling a floor before tile runs $3 to $8 per square foot installed in 2026, or $0.90 to $1.80 in material for a quarter-inch pour. The bath above needs about $282 in material: 6 bags of 50 lb underlayment, primer and lath. A floor that only reads as out of level usually needs a grind and a patch instead. The Subfloor Calculator prices the sheathing when the deck is the problem.

Flat is not level

Tile wants flat. It does not want level, and that distinction is where the money goes wrong.

ANSI A108.02 sets substrate tolerance at 1/4 in over 10 ft, with no more than 1/16 in of deviation in any 12 in span. Large format tile, any unit with an edge 15 in or longer, tightens to 1/8 in. A floor sloping an inch across a 12 ft room passes. A floor with a 3/16 in hump at the doorway fails, because the tile bridges it and leaves a void under both ends.

Self-leveling compound is named for what it does to itself. A room that slopes toward a drain still slopes after the pour.

Measure before you buy a bag

Set a 10 ft straightedge down in four directions and slide shims under the low end until it stops rocking. That stack is the depth to fill.

Two numbers matter. The deepest dip sets minimum thickness at the low point. The average depth sets the bag count, and on a single-plane slope that is half the deepest reading.

Check the deck first. A 5/8 in plywood subfloor on 16 in centers that flexes under a heel is a framing problem, and IRC Table R503.2.1(1) permits 5/8 in panels at 16 in spacing and 3/4 in at 24 in.

The bag math

Coverage on the bag is always quoted at 1/8 in. A 50 lb bag covering 50 sq ft at 1/8 in covers 25 sq ft at a quarter inch, which is where most bathroom pours land.

Primer gal = floor sq ft / 300, rounded up Bags at 1/8 in = floor sq ft / 50 Bags at depth = bags at 1/8 in x (average depth / 0.125) Buy bags = that number rounded up, plus one spare Worked example, 120 sq ft bath, average depth 1/4 in: 120 / 50 = 2.4 bags at 1/8 in 2.4 x 2 = 4.8 bags at 1/4 in, so 5, plus 1 spare 6 x $34 = $204 underlayment 1 x $38 = $38 primer, 300 sq ft per gal 1 x $40 = $40 2.5 lb lath total $282 = $2.35 per sq ft

The spare bag is not caution. A pour never lands at the average depth, and stopping short means a cold joint through the middle of the room.

Four methods, four prices

All four get called leveling. They solve different problems, and only one involves a bag of powder and a mixing barrel.

The cheapest method that hits the tolerance wins, and on an upper-floor bath that is a grind plus a patch. A quarter-inch pour over 400 sq ft runs $1,200 to $3,200, the wrong purchase when the only defect is one hump at the hallway.

Where a pour goes wrong

  • No primer. Primer costs $38, and skipping it is the most common cause of a pour that delaminates in sheets a year later.
  • A feathered edge. Most products specify 1/8 in minimum thickness, and below that the material crumbles under a trowel. Patch shallower dips first.
  • The wrong substrate. Vinyl, adhesive residue, wax and a sealed slab need removal or scarifying. A slab poured last month needs 28 days of cure.
  • Deflection left alone. Underlayment adds 2 to 3 lb per sq ft at a quarter inch and changes nothing structural. Ceramic needs L/360, stone L/720, and the Floor Joist Calculator settles the span question before anyone opens a bag.
  • Filling a control joint. Leveler over a joint is a crack waiting to telegraph through tile. Bridge it with an uncoupling membrane.

What to book

Grind and patch if the deepest dip is under 1/8 in. On that bath it is $180 to $360 and half a day.

Book the pour when the plane is off by a quarter inch or more, or when large format tile has to hit 1/8 in. The bath pour is $282 in material and $360 to $960 in labor. Skip it when the deck moves, because underlayment over a soft deck cracks along the joists within a year.

The trade-off is height and downtime. A quarter-inch pour lifts the finished floor, which means undercutting a door at $25 to $50 and a flange ring at $10 to $30, plus 16 to 24 hours before tile starts.
