Concrete Cure Time Before Framing

Published August 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Most residential builders do not need to wait the full 28 days before framing on a new foundation or slab, but you also cannot frame at the end of day one. Concrete reaches usable strength between day 3 and day 7, hits its design strength at 28 days, and keeps gaining for a year. This guide explains the 7/28/56-day strength milestones, tells you when the building code lets you frame, and shows how cold or hot weather moves the timeline in either direction.

Quick answer: Concrete typically reaches ~65% of design strength at 7 days and the full design strength at 28 days. Most residential foundations allow framing at 7 days for non-load-bearing walls and 28 days for full structural load. Verify your mix and weather with the Concrete Slab Calculator.

What "cure" actually means

Cure is the chemical reaction between Portland cement and water that turns a pour from a thick soup into rock. The reaction is not drying — it needs moisture present for weeks, not days. As long as you keep the concrete damp, the reaction continues; let it dry out and you stop gaining strength. The strength curve flattens out around 28 days under typical conditions, but concrete continues to gain small amounts of strength for a year or more.

The 7/28/56-day strength milestones

AgeApproximate % of design strengthTypical use
24 hours~15%Walk on it; do not load it
3 days~40%Light foot traffic, careful form removal
7 days~65%Most residential foundations allow framing on non-load-bearing walls
14 days~80%Driveways, light vehicle loads
28 days100% (design strength)Full structural capacity — the code baseline
56+ days105-115%Slow continued gain, relevant for high-strength or post-tensioned slabs

The percentages are typical for a standard 3,000-4,000 psi mix cured at 70°F. They shift with mix design, water/cement ratio, admixtures, and ambient temperature, so use them as a guide rather than a guarantee.

What the code says (ACI 318)

The American Concrete Institute's ACI 318 Building Code Requirements for Structural Concrete is the reference most US building departments adopt. ACI 318 lets a builder remove forms once concrete is self-supporting (typically 5 days in normal weather) and allows construction loads once compressive strength reaches at least 65% of f'c. For most residential pours that aligns with the 7-day mark for routine loads and full design strength before any heavy structural load. Always check your local code.

When can you frame?

For typical US residential construction on a standard 2,500-3,000 psi slab or footing:

  • 3-7 days: framing non-load-bearing interior partitions, drywall, mechanicals.
  • 7-10 days: framing exterior walls on a strip footing or slab-on-grade, light loads.
  • 14-28 days: backfilling foundation walls, heavy roof framing, brick or stone veneer loads.
  • 28 days: full structural capacity; the baseline for any engineered design.

These timelines assume normal 50-80°F weather with adequate moisture curing. Hot or cold weather moves the curve.

Cold weather slows it down

Cement hydration slows dramatically as temperatures drop. A 50°F pour reaches 7-day strength slower than a 70°F pour of the same mix. At 40°F concrete roughly doubles the time to the same strength; below 32°F the reaction nearly stops unless the mix is dosed with an accelerator or the slab is heated with blankets. ACI 306 recommends keeping concrete above 50°F for at least the first 3 days.

Rough rule: every 10°F drop below 70°F adds ~25-30% to the time-to-strength 50°F instead of 70°F -> 7-day becomes ~9-10 days 40°F instead of 70°F -> 7-day becomes ~14 days

Hot weather

Hot weather accelerates early strength, but it also evaporates mix water too quickly, leading to shrinkage cracks and a weaker surface. Above 90°F you need ice water in the mix, retarding admixtures, or wet curing that starts immediately. A 90°F slab may hit 7-day strength in 4-5 days, but cracking risk rises sharply without good curing.

Confirm strength before framing

  • Lab-cured cylinders: the standard evidence path. A testing lab casts cylinders at the pour and breaks them at 7 and 28 days; many building departments require this for structural footings.
  • Rebound (Schmidt) hammer: a non-destructive field check; quick and cheap but only an estimate, not a substitute for a lab test on critical work.

Cure mistakes that cost strength

  • Drying out: the #1 residential cure failure. Water the slab or cover with curing compound; otherwise strength drops 30-50%.
  • Loading too early: framing on day 1 leaves deep footprints and may over-stress the slab.
  • Too much water in the mix: every extra gallon of mix water per yard adds to workability but reduces strength by ~5%.

Plan the timeline before you pour

Decide your pour date, your framing date, and your backfill date, then size the slab, joint grid, and concrete quantity first. Enter the dimensions and the weather-adjusted cure window into the Concrete Slab Calculator so you order enough mix, and plan cure supplies before the truck arrives. For a foundation with a brick or block exterior, size the masonry separately with the block calculator so the wall schedule lines up with the cure window.
