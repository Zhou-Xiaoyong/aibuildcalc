Engineered vs Solid Hardwood Cost

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

A 1,000 sq ft ranch in Ohio gets two quotes the same week for white oak, both 4 in wide. Engineered lands at $9.40 a square foot installed. Solid lands at $12.80. Same species on the face, $3,400 apart on the invoice.

Quick answer: Engineered hardwood runs $6 to $12 per sq ft installed in 2026 and solid runs $8 to $15. Material alone is $3.50 to $9 for engineered and $4.50 to $10 for solid. The gap buys a sandable wear layer. It also buys a floor that must be nailed to a wood subfloor. The Hardwood Calculator prices either one by the room.

The gap is the wear layer, not the species

The oak on an engineered board and the oak on a solid board come off the same log. Everything under it changes.

Engineered is a plywood core, three to nine plies crossed at right angles, with a sawn face glued on. Solid is one milled board, 3/4 in thick. The crossed core keeps engineered flat through a kitchen that swings from 28% to 55% relative humidity.

Price does not track face thickness. A 7 in engineered plank with a 4 mm sawn face can cost more than #1 common oak strip, because wide faces are sorted by hand and yield per log is low.

What a sanding pass is worth

A full sand and finish runs $3.50 to $6.50 per sq ft in 2026. Whether you get to spend that money is the whole argument.

Solid 3/4 in strip leaves about 1/4 in of wood above the tongue. A drum pass takes roughly 1/32 in, so the arithmetic says seven sandings. You get two or three, because the first pass flattens cupped edges and the last cuts out old finish.

A 2 mm veneer takes one screening at most, and a screening is a buffer, not a drum. A 1 mm veneer takes none, and sanding through the face turns the board into scrap. That is what the $3.40 buys.

The 1,000 sq ft arithmetic

Waste is the first number people get wrong. A straight plank lay in a rectangle runs 5%, strip 10%, and a diagonal 15%.

Order sq ft = floor sq ft x waste factor Material = order sq ft x price per sq ft Labor = floor sq ft x install rate Trim = perimeter ft x $6 per linear ft, base and shoe Worked example, 1,000 sq ft, 40 x 25, 130 ft of perimeter: Engineered 1/2 in, nailed or floated 1,000 x 1.10 = 1,100 sq ft order 1,100 x $4.20 = $4,620 material 1,000 x $4.00 = $4,000 labor 130 x $6 = $780 trim total $9,400 = $9.40 per sq ft Solid oak strip, cleat nailed 1,000 x 1.10 = 1,100 sq ft order 1,100 x $5.80 = $6,380 material 1,000 x $5.64 = $5,640 labor, includes face nailing 130 x $6 = $780 trim total $12,800 = $12.80 per sq ft

Trim is not optional on either floor, and 3/4 in solid needs a reducer at every transition an engineered floor clears.

Most mills void the warranty on glue-down solid over concrete, and the workaround is a plywood sleeper system at $3 to $6 a square foot. In a Midwest basement the humidity swing runs wider than any solid board tolerates.

Deflection catches both. 3/4 in strip over 2x10 joists at 16 in centers wants L/360, and a 7 in plank wants L/480, so a 20 ft span with a girder at the midpoint needs checking first. The Floor Joist Calculator runs that span.

The forty-year number

Here the cheaper floor flips, and then flips back.

Two engineered floors at $9,400 each, the second at year 20, cost $18,800. One solid floor at $12,800 plus two refinishes at $4,500 reaches $21,800 over the same 40 years. Engineered wins on total outlay.

What solid buys is repair. A cast iron pan puts a gouge in a 1 mm veneer that no buffer will touch. On 3/4 in oak the same gouge sands out in an afternoon, and buyers in most Midwest markets still read solid oak as the better floor.

What to book

Go engineered, 1/2 in or thicker with a 2 mm face, on slab, below grade, over radiant heat, or in a kitchen. At $7 to $10 installed it fits those conditions without a sleeper system.

Go solid 3/4 in when the subfloor is plywood over 16 in centers, the room is above ground, and you plan to hold the house past 20 years. The cost is the nailing schedule, cleats every 6 to 8 in on the edge and 10 to 12 in in the field.

The trade-off on solid is a humidity fight. Keep the house between 30% and 50% relative humidity or the boards gap in February and crown in July. Engineered forgives that, and neither product fixes a soft subfloor. In a bedroom headed for carpet the comparison stops at the door, and the Carpet Calculator puts that floor at $2 to $8 installed.
