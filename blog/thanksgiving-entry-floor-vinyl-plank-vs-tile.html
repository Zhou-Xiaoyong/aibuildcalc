14 Pairs of Boots, 80 Sq Ft: Vinyl Plank vs. Tile Before Thanksgiving

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

An 8 by 10 foyer is 80 square feet, 88 once you add the waste, which is four cartons of 20-mil vinyl plank for $308 in material and one Saturday of work. Porcelain laid the way it has to be laid runs $1,060 to $1,760 installed and needs four days of cure before a chair goes back in. Eleven days out from the 26th, one of those schedules fits. The other one becomes a January project with a floor that is half torn up when the doorbell starts ringing.

Quick answer: 20-mil click vinyl plank, four cartons, $308, installs in six hours and takes the water. Tile is the better floor for this room over twenty years, and it is the wrong floor for the next eleven days. If you want the arithmetic for your own dimensions, put the room into the Vinyl Flooring Calculator before you order, because a 3 by 5 closet or an angled wall at the powder room changes the carton count.

Eighty square feet, measured to the door stop

Measure the room at the baseboard, not at the drywall, and measure into the closets and the powder room threshold. That is where the count drifts. A 1994 colonial foyer of 96 by 120 inches reads 80 square feet on a floor plan and 82 in the room, because the front door sits in a recess and the coat closet opens into it.

Vinyl plank is ordered with 10 percent waste, and the waste on an entry is not the waste in a bedroom. Planks run parallel to the long wall, so on an 8 by 10 room every row ends in a cut and the last row is usually under two inches wide. You also lose a plank at each doorway to the stagger. Ten percent covers it. Eight does not.

Cartons are where the estimate meets reality. Most 20-mil click products ship 20 to 24 square feet per carton, so 88 square feet is four cartons at 24.1 square feet each, or 96.4 square feet on the floor. That is 8.4 percent spare, which is one plank for the repair you will want in three years when a boot heel chips a corner. If your room has a jog in it, run the numbers the way the waste-factor guide does, wall by wall, before you round up.

Four floors, priced for 88 square feet

The entry is the one room in the house where the floor is asked to survive weather, salt, sand, and the grit that comes off a driveway. That narrows the field to two products and rules out a third outright.

Laminate is the one to strike off the list, and it is the one homeowners pick most often because it looks like the wood floor in the living room and costs half as much. The core is HDF. Water at a seam wicks into the fiber, the plank crowns within a day, and there is no repair short of replacing the row.

Sheet vinyl would go down in half a day for $440, and it is probably what is on the floor now. It fails at the edges, where a 1990s installation was glued only at the perimeter and the adhesive under the threshold has since let go.

The tile number moves with the installer, not the tile. Setting 12 by 24 porcelain runs $7 to $14 per square foot in labor in most metros, against $2 to $4 for a floating plank. The tile installation cost breakdown walks the line items if you want to argue with a quote.

Fourteen pairs of boots is a water test

Do the arithmetic before choosing, because it explains why the cheaper floor is not automatically the wrong one. A pair of insulated boots comes in carrying somewhere between a quarter and a half cup of slush on the tread and in the welt. Fourteen pairs at three-eighths of a cup is 5.25 cups, or about a third of a gallon, landing on 80 square feet in the first twenty minutes of the evening.

Then it happens again. Guests arrive Wednesday, go out Thursday morning, come back, and leave Friday. Three arrival waves at a third of a gallon is a gallon of water on the same 80 square feet in seventy-two hours, plus the road salt that comes with it. A pound of deicing salt rides in on roughly a hundred square feet of pavement, and most of it ends up in the first six feet inside the door.

Where the water goes decides the floor. Vinyl plank is waterproof as a material and not as an assembly: click seams are mechanical joints, not gaskets, so water that sits on the floor wicks through and lands on the luan underneath, where it has nowhere to go. That is survivable, because the plank will not swell and you can pull four planks, dry the subfloor, and click them back. Porcelain with epoxy grout sheds everything, but water that gets past the perimeter gap at the baseboard sits against the backer and the framing plate. Both floors want the same detail here, which is a bead of silicone at the baseboard.

What protects the floor this month is a 3 by 5 coir mat and a boot tray with a lip. Together they cost $60 and do more than the $750 spread between plank and tile.

The half inch that breaks the stair

This is the part of a tile estimate that never appears on a quote. A 12 by 24 porcelain floor over 1/4-inch cement board, with a 3/16-inch thinset bed and a 3/8-inch tile, finishes about 13/16 inch above the subfloor. Vinyl plank with an attached pad finishes at 5/16 inch. The difference is a half inch, and in a foyer it lands on two places that get inspected and one that gets noticed every day.

The stair first. If the foyer sits at the top of a basement flight, which is standard in a split level or a raised ranch, raising the floor by half an inch shortens the top riser by half an inch. Section R311.7.5 of the International Residential Code limits the difference between the tallest and shortest riser in a flight to 3/8 inch, and half an inch fails that by an eighth. The fix is a tapered shoe at the top tread or furring out the whole flight, which is a $400 to $900 change order nobody planned for.

The front door second. An exterior door set over a 1-inch threshold with a 1-inch sweep has about half an inch of clearance at the hinge side. Add tile and the door drags on the high side of the swing, which shows up as a scrape on the new floor the first time someone opens it. Check the gap before you buy the backer board.

What fits before the 26th

Two clocks run on this job, and neither is the installer's schedule. Vinyl plank wants 48 hours in the room at 65 to 85 degrees, still in the cartons, because a cold plank does not flatten and the click edges sit proud. Thinset wants the substrate at 50 degrees or better and holding there for 72 hours, and a foyer in November with a door opening thirty times a day does not hold temperature.

Two sequencing rules hold regardless of which floor you pick. Pull the baseboard and the shoe molding before the old floor comes up, because the new plank needs an expansion gap at every wall and a floor laid tight against the drywall buckles in August. And do not paint the room the same weekend. The paint film needs 48 hours above 50 degrees, and a foyer with a door propped open for a table saw will not hold it.

Thanksgiving entry floor questions

Can vinyl plank go over the existing sheet vinyl?

Sometimes, and rarely in a foyer. One layer of fully adhered, sound sheet vinyl is an approved substrate for floating plank over a wood subfloor, provided the surface is flat within 3/16 inch over 10 feet. The problem is the spot at the threshold where the old sheet has lifted, and the luan under it, which is usually stapled and soft near the door. Pull the old floor. Two hours with a pry bar is the difference between a floor that lasts and one that sounds hollow by February. If the subfloor is out of flat by more than that, the leveling guide covers the pour and the plywood patch.

Is 20 mil worth the extra dollar per square foot over 12 mil?

In this room, yes. Wear layer is the clear vinyl above the printed image, and 12 mil is a bedroom product. An entry takes grit off a driveway, and grit acts like sandpaper under a shoe. Twenty mil adds roughly $0.80 to $1.20 per square foot, or $70 to $105 on 88 square feet, and it moves the wear-through point from about year eight to beyond fifteen. The number that matters as much as wear layer is core thickness, which should be 5 mm or better at the click edge.

Do I need a transition strip at the powder room door?

Yes, and it should be a T-molding rather than a reducer if both floors finish at similar heights. A 2-inch-wide T covers the expansion gap on both sides without trapping the plank, and it takes the traffic that would otherwise catch a plank edge. Leave 3/8 inch of gap under the molding on each side. A reducer is for the drop to carpet, so if the foyer opens to a carpeted living room, that is a second piece you need.

The call

Buy four cartons of 20-mil click plank, a roll of 6-mil poly for the subfloor, two T-moldings, and a tube of silicone. That is $382 at the register and one Saturday, and it leaves you a spare plank for the chip that will happen in year three. Start by the 18th so the cartons have their 48 hours in a warm room before you open them.

The trade-off is honest. Porcelain is the floor this foyer deserves: it outlives the house, shrugs off salt, and does not care how many boots come through. It also costs $1,060 to $1,760, eats four days of cure, and quietly raises the floor half an inch against a stair with a 3/8-inch tolerance. If the stair is on the spring list anyway, tile it in April and put a mat down this month. If the foyer just needs to stop looking ashamed when the doorbell rings, the plank wins every number that matters this week.
