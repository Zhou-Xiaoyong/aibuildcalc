Exterior Paint Calculator with Doors and Windows

Published September 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Exterior painting estimates go wrong in one of two directions. Count the walls and forget the openings, and you buy far more paint than the siding will ever absorb. Subtract every window and door as if the siding behind them were missing, and you come up short with a half-painted gable end. The trick is subtracting openings the way a painter actually does, then adjusting for the texture of the siding.

Quick answer: Measure the gross wall area, subtract 15 to 20 square feet for each window and 20 to 21 square feet for each exterior door, then divide by a coverage figure that matches your siding. Smooth lap siding covers about 300 to 350 square feet per gallon per coat; rough or textured siding drops to 200 to 250. Work it out siding by siding with the Exterior Paint Calculator.

Step 1: gross wall area

Walk the house and measure each wall separately, length times height from the foundation line to the soffit. Do not include the roof gables at the same time, because their triangular area is easier to figure on its own.

Gross wall area = sum of (wall length x wall height) Gable area = (Triangle base x Triangle height) / 2 Openings (sq ft) = (Windows x 15) + (Doors x 21) Paintable siding = Gross area - Openings Gallons per coat = Paintable siding / Coverage per gallon Total gallons = Gallons per coat x Coats

The two deductions above are working averages. A standard single-hung window is often 3 feet by 5 feet on the outside, or about 15 square feet; a 3/0 by 6/8 entry door is close to 21 square feet including the frame. Count a window with a wide surround and shutters a little higher, a small bathroom window lower. Precision here matters less than consistency, because these are small fractions of the wall.

Step 2: how much to subtract for openings

Here is where most estimates go wrong, and it is worth understanding why the calculator does not simply remove every opening in full.

OpeningActual areaRecommended deductionWhy the difference
Standard window~15 sq ft15 sq ftClose to actual; trim and sill are painted separately
Large picture window30-45 sq ftHalf the areaSurround, flashing and casing still get painted
Exterior door, solid~21 sq ft21 sq ftThe slab usually gets painted, so count it back in
Sliding glass door~42 sq ft30 sq ftFrame and track surround are still painted
Garage door, 16 ft~112 sq ft0 if paintedA painted garage door is a large surface of its own

Treat a painted garage door or painted entry slab as a separate line rather than a deduction. Both are large flat surfaces that take real paint, and if you subtract them from the wall you will double-count the savings.

Step 3: match coverage to the siding

Coverage is the variable that moves the answer most, because exterior paints are engineered for specific substrates and textures. The figures below are per gallon, per coat, from real-world application rather than the best case on the label.

Siding typeCoverage per gallonNotes
Smooth lap or hardboard siding300-350 sq ftIdeal; primer and paint both go further
Beaded or rough-sawn lap200-275 sq ftTexture doubles the surface area in practice
Stucco, smooth finish200-300 sq ftVery absorbent; a masonry primer helps
Cedar shake or shingle150-250 sq ftDeep texture; often brushed or sprayed and back-brushed
Brick or concrete block150-250 sq ftPorous and irregular; elastomeric products are common
Vinyl or aluminum siding350-400 sq ftSmooth and sealed; the best coverage of any substrate

Step 4: coats and the one-body-shop rule

Two coats is the standard recommendation for an exterior repaint and non-negotiable for a color change. A single coat of exterior paint thinned for spray application will not deliver the film thickness that resists weather. Buy all buckets from the same batch, because exterior color matching is done at the factory and an odd bucket can flash noticeably on a large sunny wall. Spraying is fast but consumes 15 to 25 percent more paint than brushing and rolling, so if your painter sprays, add that waste factor in.

A worked example

A single-story house, 60 feet by 30 feet, with 9 foot walls. The gross wall area is 2 x (60 + 30) x 9, or 1,620 square feet. It has 14 windows at roughly 15 square feet and two doors at 21, or about 252 square feet of openings, leaving 1,368 square feet of paintable siding. On smooth lap siding at 320 square feet per gallon per coat, one coat takes 4.3 gallons, two coats take 8.6, and rounding up with a 10 percent cushion gives 10 gallons. If that same house were cedar shake at 200 square feet per gallon, the total would climb to about 14 gallons, which is exactly why the calculator asks for siding type rather than assuming one number.

What the calculator still cannot see

  • Failing paint and bare wood. Areas that need scraping, sanding, and priming are extra labor, often the largest line in an exterior job.
  • Caulking and rot repair. Every joint and gap should be caulked before the finish coat; soft trim has to be replaced first.
  • Trim, fascia and soffits. These are painted separately and often in a contrasting color; measure them on their own.
  • Access. Two-story walls, steep lots, and roof valleys that need a ladder platform add labor, not paint.

From gallons to a bid

Once you have the gallon count, turn it into a price with a per-gallon material figure and the labor rate for exterior work.
