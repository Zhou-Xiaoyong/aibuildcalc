Where Do 6 Cars Park on Thanksgiving? 648 Sq Ft and 12 Tons of Gravel

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Fourteen guests arrive in six cars. Your driveway holds two. The other four need 648 square feet of ground that will not turn to mud, which is four stalls at 9 by 18 feet, 8 cubic yards of crushed stone at 4 inches, or 12 tons delivered. Material runs $500 to $820. Tuesday afternoon is enough time to spread it.

Quick answer: four side-by-side stalls are 36 feet of frontage by 18 feet deep. At 4 inches compacted that is 8.0 cubic yards and 10.8 tons of #57 crushed stone, ordered as 12 tons with a 10 percent allowance for low spots. Add woven geotextile underneath and you are at $700 to $1,120 for a DIY job one person can finish in four hours. The Gravel Calculator does the same arithmetic for your own width and depth.

Four stalls is 36 feet, not 40

Residential stall dimensions barely move: 9 feet wide by 18 feet deep for a standard car, 8.5 feet as the tight end for a full-size pickup with both doors open. Four stalls laid edge to edge measure 36 feet across the front and 18 feet in depth. That is 648 square feet, and it is the number to take to the stone yard.

Where the pad sits decides if you need an aisle. Beside the driveway, the driveway is the aisle and the stone starts at the pavement edge. Behind a garage, add 24 feet of clear turning space or the fourth car will not get out on Saturday morning without a three-point turn through the flower bed.

Count cars honestly before you order. Six cars for fourteen people is the usual ratio, and one of those cars leaves at nine in the evening, so the peak is five. Build four stalls and accept that Thursday evening has a shuffle in it.

The stone math, step by step

Crushed stone is sold by the ton in most of the country and by the cubic yard in the Northeast. Both numbers come off the same volume, and the conversion is where estimates go wrong. At the 100 pounds per cubic foot density that #57 stone typically runs, one cubic yard weighs 2,700 pounds, or 1.35 tons.

4 stalls, 4 inches compacted Pad area 4 stalls x 9 ft x 18 ft = 648 sq ft Volume 648 x (4 / 12) = 216 cu ft 216 / 27 = 8.0 cu yd Weight 8.0 cu yd x 1.35 tons/cu yd = 10.8 tons Order quantity, 10% for low spots 8.0 x 1.10 = 8.8 cu yd 10.8 x 1.10 = 11.9 tons -> order 12 Material cost, 2026 delivered 12 tons x $42 to $68/ton = $504 to $816 At 6 inches instead of 4 648 x 0.5 / 27 = 12.0 cu yd -> 16.2 tons with waste: 13.2 cu yd, 17.8 tons -> order 18

Order the 12 tons, not 10.8. The extra 1.2 tons is a quarter inch of average depth, which is what disappears into the first low corner you find under a headlight. Most yards run a 10-ton minimum on delivery and charge a $75 to $125 short-load fee below it, so a 12-ton load costs almost nothing more than a 10-ton one and arrives on a single truck.

The concrete row is the one that ends the argument. Ready-mix needs seven days at 50 degrees or better before it takes a car, and late November does not offer that in most of the country. You cannot pour Tuesday and park Thursday. Pavers are a spring project too, because a plate compactor will not seat bedding sand that is frozen or saturated.

Millings deserve a second look on price alone, at roughly a third of the stone cost. They also need a hot day and a roller to knit together. Cold millings spread in November stay loose, and every guest walks them into the kitchen. The black footprints on a beige hallway cost more to clean than the $400 you saved.

Four inches or six

Four inches of #57 is a one-season pad. #57 has no fines to bind it, so the surface ravels under turning tires and the edges creep outward a few inches a year. For a holiday overflow pad that gets used four days a year, that is the right trade. For a pad you intend to keep, six inches is the number, and it costs $250 to $400 more in stone.

Two things matter more than the depth. First, strip 4 inches of topsoil before the stone goes down. Stone spread over sod pumps into the soft layer below it and disappears within one wet spring. Second, lay woven geotextile over the stripped subgrade with 12-inch overlaps. A 15 by 60 foot roll covers 900 square feet for $85 to $140 and is the difference between stone that stays at 4 inches and stone that needs re-topping every other year.

Grade the finished surface 2 percent away from the house. Twelve tons of stone piled against a foundation pushes water toward the sill, and most inspectors want 6 inches of foundation visible below the siding for exactly that reason. If your siding already sits close to grade, keep the stone 3 feet back from the wall and let that strip drain to the lawn.

The 48-hour version, hour by hour

Guests land Wednesday afternoon. That leaves today and tomorrow. The sequence is short enough to run without a contractor, provided you make the phone call first.

  • Monday, before 2 p.m. Call two yards and ask for #57, 12 tons, delivered Tuesday morning. Ask where they can drop it: a tailgate spread along the length of the pad saves an hour of wheelbarrow work, and a single pile in the middle means moving 12 tons twice.
  • Monday evening. Buy or reserve a plate compactor for Tuesday, $75 to $110 for a day. Pick up the geotextile and a steel bow rake. Rent nothing else.
  • Tuesday morning. Strip 4 inches of sod and topsoil across 36 by 18 feet, and grade it 2 percent away from the house. Two people, about 90 minutes with a shovel and a wheelbarrow.
  • Tuesday midday. Roll out the fabric, overlap the seams a foot, and turn the edges under the stone so they do not show. Then two 2-inch lifts, compacting each one before the next goes down. Compacting 4 inches at once leaves the bottom half loose.
  • Tuesday afternoon. Rake the surface flat, compact a final pass, and park a car on it to check the grade. Water should run off, not pool.

Expect the stone to feel loose for two weeks. It is. The first rain settles the surface and the second one locks it. Do not top it off in that window; you will overfill and shovel it back out in April.

The version that costs more than the stone

Parking on the lawn looks free until March. A tire's contact pressure is essentially its inflation pressure, 33 to 36 psi for a passenger car, and a wet November lawn gives way at a small fraction of that. One car for one evening leaves 3 to 5 inch ruts. Four cars for three days leaves a repair bill of $200 to $500 in topsoil, seed and a rented roller, and the lawn does not look right again until June.

Plywood runners are the honest fallback if the stone does not arrive. Two sheets of 3/4-inch plywood, $55 to $75 each, laid where the tires sit, hold a car for one night on ground that is merely damp. They will not survive a soaking rain, and they slide on a slope.

One place never to put the pad: over a septic drainfield or within 10 feet of a tank. Twelve tons of stone and six parked cars compact the soil above the laterals and crush the pipe. That turns a $500 parking problem into a $12,000 system replacement, which is a bad trade even by holiday standards. If the only flat ground on the property is the drainfield, park on the street and shuttle. The septic capacity math for 18 guests is worth reading before you decide the lawn is expendable.

Thanksgiving parking questions

How many cars fit on 648 square feet of gravel?

Four, at 9 by 18 feet each, parked nose-in and side by side. If two of the cars are full-size pickups, plan on three comfortable stalls and one tight one, because a 6.5-foot bed adds roughly 2 feet of length and the doors need room to open.

Can I spread stone over the grass instead of stripping it?

For one weekend, yes, with 4 inches and fabric between the sod and the stone. It will survive Thursday and look like a mistake by spring, because the grass underneath decays and the stone sinks into the void it leaves. Stripping costs 90 minutes and saves the whole job.

Will gravel get dragged into the garage or the house?

Loose stone travels on shoes. A 4-inch pressure-treated edge board or a single row of edging pavers on the walking side contains most of it, and a 2 percent slope away from the doors handles the rest. A boot tray inside the door catches what is left. The Edging Calculator gives you the linear feet for whatever border you pick.

The call

Order 12 tons of #57 today and skip both the pavers and the concrete. Spread it in two lifts over woven fabric, keep it 3 feet off the siding, and you have four stalls for $700 and one afternoon of work.

The trade-off is real: 4 inches of loose stone is a one-season answer. It will ravel at the edges and want 2 tons of re-top within two years. If you already know the pad is permanent, order 18 tons now and put 6 inches down in one delivery. Paying for the second load later costs the same stone price plus another delivery fee, and the second delivery is the part that stings.
