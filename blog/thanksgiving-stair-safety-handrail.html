Will Your Stairs Handle 14 Thanksgiving Guests? 480 Footfalls and a $410 Fix

Published November 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Eight risers, sixty-one inches of rise, and a bottom step that stands 7/8 inch taller than the seven above it. Fourteen people will cross that flight sixty times on the 26th, which works out to 480 footfalls in about three hours, on a stair whose handrail is a flat 1x4 screwed to the wall at 31 inches. The rail, the nosing strips and two step lights cost $410 in parts. The riser geometry is a January job with a saw in it.

Quick answer: the flight passes on tread depth at 10 inches and fails on four other counts. The bottom riser measures 8-3/8 inches against a legal maximum of 7-3/4, the flat 1x4 rail sits at 31 inches when the code wants 34 to 38, and a 200-pound pull at midspan puts 5,335 psi of bending stress into a board rated for about 1,100. Parts for a graspable rail, 15 nosing strips and two step lights total $410, installed cost for the rail alone runs $650 to $1,200. Measure your own rise and run with the Wood Stair Calculator before you buy anything.

Where Thanksgiving traffic actually goes

The dining room seats eight. The other six eat at a folding table in the family room downstairs, and that one fact puts the whole party on the stair. Coats go to the basement closet, the extra pies cool on the landing, and somebody's uncle wants the game, which is downstairs too.

Count it honestly. Six diners down and back is 12 trips. Eight upstairs guests each making one trip to see the family room is 16. Three kids at four round trips each is 24. Platters carried between the two levels adds 8. Sixty trips across eight risers is 480 footfalls, between the first knock at the door and the last car leaving.

A family of four runs that stair about 28 times on a weekday. The party does 60 trips in three hours, after dark, with a plate in each hand.

The bottom step grew by seven-eighths of an inch

Split-levels and raised ranches built between 1955 and 1970 pick this up by accident. The basement went in with vinyl over a 1/4-inch underlayment, somebody pulled that floor up two summers ago, and the slab at the foot of the stair now sits 7/8 inch below where the carpenter assumed it would be in 1962.

The seven upper risers are still 7-1/2 inches, cut into the stringer with nothing to move them. The bottom riser takes up the difference at 8-3/8, and total rise is 60-7/8 inches, which is what eight risers of 7.6 inches gives you anyway. Nothing looks wrong until the level goes on the second tread.

IRC R311.7.5.1 caps a riser at 7-3/4 inches and sets tread depth at 10 inches minimum. It also says the tallest riser in a flight cannot exceed the shortest by more than 3/8 inch. Seven-eighths against three-eighths is 2.3 times the allowance, on the first step every guest meets.

You cannot shim out of it. A 7/8-inch pad or a doubled bottom tread swaps one violation for another, because the minimum riser is 4 inches and a 7/8-inch step at the foot of a flight is a toe-catcher that fails the same table. The real fix is a riser and 10 inches of run cut into the stringers, $900 to $1,800 for a carpenter, and not this month.

What the tape measure finds against the code

Ten minutes with a tape and a level turns a vague worry into a list. Measure riser height at the front edge of each tread, then measure the rail height off the nosing, not off the floor. Those two numbers catch most of what goes wrong on a basement flight.

Four failures and one pass. Tread depth is fine, which is the number most people check and the one that is almost never wrong on a house this age. The rail is the problem: 31 inches is low enough that a hand rests on top of it instead of wrapping around it, and a 1x4 is 3/4 inch thick, under the 1-1/4-inch minimum for a round profile and too small in perimeter to qualify as a Type II shape.

A graspable rail lets a hand close on it, so a stumble becomes a catch. A flat board gives fingers nothing to hook, and sliding a palm along the top does nothing at all when a foot misses a step.

What a 200-pound pull does to a 1x4 rail

IRC Table R301.5 and IBC 1607.8.1 both call for a rail to resist a 200-pound concentrated load applied at any point and in any direction. That is not a body falling against it. It is a large adult losing their footing and grabbing.

Run it as a simple beam between two brackets 35 inches apart, which is what you get when brackets land on studs at 16 inches on center with one skipped. A 200-pound point load at midspan produces 1,750 pound-inches of moment. A 1x4 flat, actual 3/4 by 3-1/2, has a section modulus of 0.328 cubic inches, so the bending stress is 5,335 psi against an allowable near 1,100 for oak. The board is asked for almost five times what it can give.

In practice the wood does not snap. The rail bows about an inch, the two screws at the nearest bracket tear through 3/4 inch of oak, and the whole thing comes off the wall in one piece. A rail that feels solid under a hand push is not the same as one that passes a grab.

More brackets do not rescue that board. At 16 inches on center, which is every stud, the same 1x4 flat still computes to 2,440 psi, so there is no spacing that turns it into a compliant rail. The profile has to change. A 2-1/4 inch round oak rail over the same 35-inch span has 3.4 times the section modulus and drops the pull to 1,565 psi, which the short-duration impact factor covers for the second or two that a grab lasts.

The other side of the flight is a bare wall

A dwelling needs a handrail on one side of a flight with four or more risers, so this stair is legal even at 31 inches. The second rail is a comfort item until somebody in the party is over 75, and then it is the difference between a normal evening and an ambulance. Same 8-foot board, eight brackets, two returns: $216 in parts and three hours. Do it if two guests use a cane. Skip it on a 36-inch flight, where two rails leave about 31 inches of clear width between them.

Light does half the work

NEC 210.70 requires a lighting outlet for a stairway with six or more risers, switched at each floor level. An eight-riser flight qualifies, and this one has a single bulb at the top on a single switch, which means the last person up turns off the light while somebody is still on the bottom three steps.

The photometry is the part nobody checks. A 60-watt equivalent lamp in an open fixture at the top of the flight puts 3 to 5 footcandles on the upper treads and 1 to 2 on the bottom step, because illuminance falls off with the square of the distance and the bottom step is about 14 feet from the bulb at a shallow angle. A nosing is 1 inch deep. At 1 footcandle you cannot see where the tread ends.

Two surface-mounted LED step lights, 12 inches above the bottom tread, bring that to 8 to 10 footcandles for about $149 with a short run of 14/2 and a switch. Surface raceway avoids opening the drywall, which matters in a week with no time to patch and paint. The Lighting Calculator sizes the fixture count if the flight runs a full storey instead of a half.

The nosing strips are the cheapest line on the list and the one that speaks to the actual defect. You are not allowed to leave a 7/8-inch step at the bottom of a flight, but you are not rebuilding it in six days either, so the honest move is to make the drop visible: a 2-inch strip of grit-backed aluminum in a contrasting color on the front edge of the bottom tread, and on the top tread where the light is behind you.

If the guests are sleeping in the family room instead of driving home, the stair is not the only code question downstairs. A sleeping room in a basement needs egress, which is a window well and a 5.7 sq ft opening. The egress window cost breakdown covers what that runs and why the permit is the long pole.

Six days out, in this order

  • Measure every riser at the nosing and write the numbers down. If the spread is under 3/8 inch, the stair is fine and the rest of this is comfort work.
  • Pull hard on the rail at the middle of the flight, straight out from the wall, with both hands. Any movement at the bracket means the screws are in drywall, and eight new brackets is $48.
  • Replace the rail if it is a flat 1x4 at 31 inches. That is the $410 job, and it is the only item on this list that changes a code failure into a pass.
  • Swap the single top bulb for two step lights and a three-way switch at the bottom, or accept that half the party walks down a dark flight with a plate.
  • Skip the stair gate, the carpet runner and the motion-sensor night light. None of them changes a load path or a footcandle, and the runner on a 10-inch tread with a 1-inch nosing is its own trip hazard.

If a contractor is coming anyway, get the riser rebuild priced at the same visit. The carpenter who wants $650 to $1,200 for the rail will want $900 to $1,800 to cut a riser and extend the run 10 inches, and one mobilization beats two. Thanksgiving stair questions

Does a basement stair need a handrail on both sides?

One side is enough in a house. The residential code asks for a handrail on at least one side of a flight with four or more risers, and it does not require a second one unless the stair is open on that side. Two rails on a 36-inch-wide flight leave too little clear width to walk comfortably, which is why most basement flights have one.

How high should a handrail be on a stair?

34 to 38 inches, measured vertically from the nosing of the tread below to the top of the rail. Measuring from the floor is the common mistake and it reads 3 to 4 inches high on a flight with 10-inch treads, because the nose sits forward of the riser. The rail on this stair measures 31 inches off the nose, which is below the range and the reason a hand slides along the top instead of gripping it.

Will 14 people standing on the stairs overload them?

No. A residential stair is designed for 40 psf of live load plus a 300-pound concentrated load on a single tread, and eight people on one flight is a fraction of that. The failure that happens at a party is a missed step and a hand that finds nothing to hold. If the flight feels bouncy or the treads squeal, that is deflection and loose shims, worth a look from underneath once the holiday is over.

The call

Buy the $410 parts list and spend Saturday on the rail first and the step lights second. A graspable rail at 36 inches with brackets into studs every 32 inches turns the one part of this stair that fails a 200-pound pull into the strongest thing on the flight.

The trade-off is the bottom riser, which stays at 8-3/8 inches until someone cuts the stringers. Nosing strips make it visible and nothing more, so put your oldest guest at the top of the flight and keep a hand free on the way down. Book the rebuild for January, when $900 to $1,800 buys a proper 7-1/2-inch step and the run to go with it. The floor load check for 14 guests answers the other Thanksgiving question worth settling before the 26th.
