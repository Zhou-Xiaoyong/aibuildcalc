How to Estimate Paint for Ceilings

Published October 2026 · 8 min read · By AIBuildCalc

Ceilings look harder to estimate than walls and are actually easier, because the surface area is sitting on the floor plan. The complications are limited to two things: how a sloped or vaulted ceiling changes the area, and how much more paint an overhead surface uses than the coverage table suggests.

Quick answer: For a flat ceiling, the square footage to paint equals the floor area of the room. Multiply by two coats and divide by roughly 350 to 400 square feet per gallon. A 12 by 12 room needs about three quarters of a gallon per coat, so one gallon covers a standard two-coat ceiling job. Estimate your rooms with the Paint Calculator.

Ceiling area is floor area

A flat ceiling is a mirror of the floor beneath it, so a 12 by 12 foot room has 144 square feet of ceiling and a 20 by 15 foot living room has 300. Measure the room at the widest point and, if the shape is irregular, break it into rectangles and add them up. Do not subtract light fixtures, smoke detectors or vents; they remove a trivial amount of surface and their trim still needs cutting in.

Flat ceiling area = Room length x Room width Sloped ceiling area = Plan area / cos(Pitch angle) or, in the field: Sloped area = Plan area x Slope factor Cost = Ceiling area x (Paint per sq ft + Labor per sq ft)

Sloped, vaulted and cathedral ceilings

A pitched ceiling is longer on the surface than it is on the plan, so the flat-area shortcut undercounts. The correction is the slope factor: divide the plan area by the cosine of the pitch angle, or use the field multipliers below.

A cathedral ceiling can add 30 to 40 percent to the ceiling area of the same room, which is the difference between one gallon and two. Cathedral ceilings also usually bring taller walls and a second-story void, so the total room area grows twice over.

How much paint a ceiling actually uses

Ceilings are usually painted flat or with a dedicated ceiling paint, and both cover well on a previously painted surface. They cover much less on new drywall or bare joint compound, which is absorbing the first coat rather than holding it.

Surface and productCoverage per gallonNote
Previously painted, ceiling flat350-400 sq ftThe best case, one coat often enough
Previously painted, dedicated ceiling paint350-400 sq ftThicker body, less splatter
New drywall, first coat200-300 sq ftAbsorbs heavily at joints
Water stain or smoke damage150-250 sq ftNeeds a stain-blocking primer first
Popcorn or heavy texture250-300 sq ftMore surface area per square foot

Overhead work also wastes more paint than wall work. Roller splatter, drips from an overloaded cover, and the need to reload more often at shoulder height all push real consumption a little above the label figure, which is why a small margin matters more on ceilings than on walls.

The practical takeaway: a single bedroom ceiling is a one-gallon job and a whole house of ceilings is a five-gallon pail, so ceilings are rarely the thing that breaks the budget. They are, however, the thing that most often gets skipped, and a bright white ceiling is an inexpensive way to make a repainted room look finished.

One coat or two on a ceiling

If the ceiling is already white and in good condition, one careful coat is often enough, especially with a dedicated ceiling paint formulated to hide roller marks. If it is stained, yellowed, patched, or being changed from a color to white, two coats over a primer is the only way to get an even result. Water stains need a shellac or oil-based stain blocker before any latex ceiling paint, or the stain will bleed back through within weeks.

What ceiling painting costs if you hire it

Professional ceiling painting runs about $0.80 to $2.00 per square foot of ceiling, which is higher than walls because overhead work is slower and harder on the crew. A standard bedroom ceiling typically comes in at $150 to $300 when bundled with wall painting, and $250 to $500 as a standalone job, since rolling a single small ceiling rarely fills a day but still carries a minimum charge. Move furniture, cover the floor and remove light fixtures before the crew arrives and you will not pay for it.

Measure each room once, note which ceilings are flat and which are vaulted, then run the numbers.
